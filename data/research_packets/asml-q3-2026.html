What customers bought

One reportable segment; no invented product profit allocation.

ASML reports one consolidated segment. Systems generated €8.373 billion, while service and field options generated €3.289 billion. Installed Base Management is the company’s label for that service-and-options category. It rose 67.6% year over year and represented 28.2% of total sales. The label should not be read as a pure subscription stream: productivity upgrades can be substantial, uneven transactions. Management attributes the margin outperformance to strong productivity-upgrade demand.

The system count increased to 121, including 113 new and eight used machines. That provides a physical cross-check on the revenue ramp, but it is not an average-selling-price model: technology mix, revenue recognition and upgrade sales differ across periods. High-NA EUV, conventional EUV, immersion and other tools have very different economics. The correct inference is stronger throughput, not a claim that every machine became more profitable by the same percentage.

The longer-term opportunity is the interaction between customer capacity and lithography intensity. More demanding chip designs can require additional exposures, and upgrades can add wafer capacity without a complete new fab build. The announced TSMC photomask initiative targets a productivity increase exceeding 40% for dual-mask exposure. That is a technology objective, not a realized increase in consolidated revenue or margin. We give it strategic relevance but no separate dollar value in the base case.

Follow the profit

Operating improvement is real; below-the-line gains still need separation.

Net income of €4.960 billion includes a €509.7 million gain on equity investments. That gain accounts for 8.9% of pretax income. The cash-flow statement reverses €508.8 million related to equity investments, supporting the conclusion that most of this contribution is noncash in the quarter. It should not be capitalized as recurring machine earnings. The €134.7 million equity-method contribution is another separate source of profit and should not be confused with cash dividends received.

For an illustrative sensitivity, applying the quarter’s effective tax rate to the investment gain would reduce net income to approximately €4.533 billion. That is our computation, not management’s adjusted EPS and not a claim about the gain’s actual tax treatment. The resulting profit would still be well above the prior year. The analytical conclusion is therefore two-part: the operating advance survives normalization, but the headline net-income growth exaggerates the recurring improvement.

Operating cash flow reached €11.798 billion, but €7.009 billion came from changes in operating assets and liabilities. That is 59.4% of the cash inflow. Removing that contribution mechanically leaves €4.789 billion of operating cash and €4.341 billion after both forms of capital expenditure. These are diagnostic sensitivities, not alternative GAAP measures and not estimates of exactly repeatable cash flow. They show why annualizing the reported cash figure would be a serious mistake.

Long-term contract liabilities rose by €5.730 billion sequentially to €9.301 billion. This supports the interpretation that customers are helping finance future deliveries, but the summary does not provide a complete current contract-liability bridge. We therefore do not assert that every euro of the working-capital inflow was a customer deposit. Receivables also increased, and other liabilities can contribute. The liability represents an obligation to deliver future value; it is not free distributable profit.

The nine-quarter record reveals the seasonality: a large year-end inflow can be followed by a cash-consuming first quarter. The current trailing cash total is stronger than the annual history, but remains affected by customer payment timing. We do not compute a Beneish or Altman score from incomplete current liability and retained-earnings detail. A numerical score built from guessed inputs would add confidence without information. The useful questions are whether receivables convert, the advance-funded equipment is delivered, and margins persist when the cash cycle normalizes.

At our 30× multiple, the reference price requires about €51.47 of earnings per share. At a 35% net margin and the same share count, that translates to approximately €56.3 billion of annual revenue. The cash-flow route is deliberately less enthusiastic than the earnings routes. Its terminal value contributes approximately 82% of total value, making both the discount rate and the long-run growth assumption important. A permanent 4% terminal growth rate is already demanding for a large equipment company. The higher earnings values depend on investors continuing to award a premium multiple to the technology position. These methods share economic assumptions; agreement is a cross-check, not three independent proofs.

The bull case deserves respect: capacity remains scarce, productivity upgrades support margins, and the installed base can generate growth even when new-machine output is constrained. Our restraint comes from the price, the customer concentration and the time needed to deliver the expansion. Conversely, a HOLD is not a prediction of operating deterioration. The annual report describes ordinary shares with one vote each and no issued cumulative preference shares. A preference-share foundation has a protective option, which matters for takeover governance even though it is not current ordinary-share dilution. Nasdaq valuation uses the ordinary-share economic interest without introducing an unsupported ADS conversion ratio. The auditor is PwC, whose annual opinion states that it has served since 2024. An audit opinion is assurance over historical statements, not insurance against a future capacity shortfall.

A particularly important supplier relationship is Carl Zeiss SMT. ASML owns 24.9% of its holding entity and relies on it for optical columns. The annual report classifies that entity as a variable interest entity but says ASML is not the primary beneficiary; it uses equity-method accounting. The relationship includes financing and prepayment obligations. It should therefore be understood as a concentrated strategic dependency, not described as either wholly owned supply or a nonexistent VIE exposure.

The strongest bull argument is that conventional equipment-cycle valuation underestimates a durable bottleneck. If customers continue to increase lithography spending, upgrades monetize the installed base and capacity comes online on schedule, both earnings and the appropriate multiple could exceed our assumptions. The bull scenario does not require the current investment gain to recur. It requires stronger recurring sales and margins, with a market still willing to pay a premium for them.

The strongest bear argument is that today’s visibility reflects synchronized customer optimism near the top of a capital-spending cycle. Commitments can slip, export permissions can change, and machinery built for an aggressive ramp can leave inventory and cash tied up. A high-margin quarter followed by lower utilization would compress earnings and the multiple together. Customer advances soften near-term funding needs, but they do not remove the obligation to build and deliver the tools.

Our position sits between those outcomes. We accept the operating improvement and the strategic strength, but do not treat either as permission to ignore valuation. The wide scenario range is intentional: this is a high-quality business with uncertain timing and a demanding stock price. Position size, time horizon and existing exposure matter to an investor’s decision; the published HOLD is a company-level research judgment, not a personalized trade instruction.
