The tape

The print

Growth is strong; the guidance bridge is stronger

Revenue increased to $6.173 billion from $4.441 billion, while adjusted EBITDA rose to $1.900 billion from $1.608 billion. The different growth rates matter. Higher gas-linked revenue does not translate dollar for dollar into retained economics because feedstock costs and variable contract pricing move with it. Cargoes increased to 188 from 163, which supplies physical evidence that the quarter was more than a price effect. Cheniere DCF was $1.230 billion, lower than the unusually strong year-ago $1.610 billion. The prior quarter’s cash-tax benefit makes a simple DCF growth ranking misleading.

The current earnings snapshot puts reported revenue above the provider’s $5.365 billion estimate and diluted EPS of $5.65 above $4.33. These comparisons are useful context, but the EPS line is not a clean operating beat: derivative accounting can move GAAP profit sharply. Neither the release nor this research equates a larger GAAP surprise with a proportionate increase in sustainable cash. The business test is whether physical output, contract economics and capital demands jointly support cash available to the common shareholder.

The new midpoint requires fourth-quarter adjusted EBITDA approximately 56% above Q3 and Cheniere DCF approximately 81% above Q3. That does not automatically make the guide implausible: additional operating capacity, delivery timing and marketing economics can produce uneven quarters. It does make the guide a concentrated execution claim. A calendar-year annualization of the latest quarter would miss that concentration. At the low end, Q4 still needs $2.713 billion of adjusted EBITDA and $1.980 billion of DCF. Those are the first checkpoints we will use at the next print.

Management has already raised the full-year range, so the bear case cannot simply assume management forgot the ramp. Our disagreement is about how much recurring value the market should assign before that ramp converts to durable common-owner cash. Strong fourth-quarter delivery would validate this year’s numerical outlook. It would not, by itself, prove that a favorable marketing spread or tax timing benefit should be capitalized forever. The valuation below deliberately distinguishes a good current year from a repeatable through-cycle cash base.

Business economics

One reportable segment, two major physical systems

Cheniere reports one operating segment. Sabine Pass and Corpus Christi are important operating systems and legal entities, but they are not separately reported GAAP profit segments in this packet. Creating a terminal-profit chart from guessed allocations would make the analysis look more complete while making it less reliable. Instead, we show the reported revenue categories, consolidated profit and physical cargo data, then explain the ownership structure that determines who ultimately receives the cash.

LNG revenue was $6.099 billion, regasification revenue $34 million and other revenue $40 million. The LNG category dominates, so visual similarity between the total-revenue and LNG-revenue charts is economically accurate. Long-term contracts provide a base of contracted economics, while variable fees, gas costs and marketing exposure create additional variation. The current filing says 73% of LNG revenue under the relevant customer contracts was variable consideration in Q3. That percentage is not the same as uncontracted merchant exposure: indexed fees can be embedded in long-term agreements.

The current filing describes total expected capacity above 60 mtpa including estimated debottlenecking opportunities, with roughly 5 mtpa still under construction. That is a capacity statement, not a promise that every tonne is sold at a fixed margin tomorrow. The release reports additional Corpus Christi Stage 3 progress, including substantial completion of Train 7 in August. Commissioning, commercial service and full-year cash contribution are different dates, which is why the fourth-quarter bridge matters more than merely counting trains.

The competitive advantage is the combination of existing infrastructure, contracted relationships, access to U.S. gas and operating experience. The constraint is equally physical: an outage cannot be fixed by changing a software release, and replacement cargoes or shipping arrangements can be costly. Growth projects can improve scale while increasing the cash tied up before the benefits arrive. We therefore give operational growth credit, but demand evidence that higher volumes translate into cash after the construction and minority layers.

Profit bridge

The reported earnings line is not the cash line

Third-quarter operating income of $1.915 billion became $1.654 billion of pretax income after net below-the-line expense. Tax expense of $168 million left $1.486 billion of consolidated net income. Non-controlling interests received $316 million of that profit, leaving $1.170 billion attributable to Cheniere. This sequence is why applying a multiple to consolidated net income and dividing by parent shares would overstate the common shareholder’s claim. The NCI deduction is an ownership allocation, not optional conservatism.

The nine-month picture is markedly different. Parent net income was only $736 million even though adjusted EBITDA was $6.037 billion. Q1 included a large GAAP loss, followed by a large Q2 recovery. Those swings should lead the analyst to the derivative notes rather than to an invented collapse and recovery in physical operations. They also explain why a simple trailing GAAP P/E is a poor standalone valuation anchor for this print.

Adjusted EBITDA and DCF are useful precisely because the GAAP line can be volatile, but useful does not mean complete. EBITDA excludes financing, taxes and the capital needed to keep or expand the plants. Cheniere DCF subtracts specified cash and maintenance items and attributes the result to Cheniere, yet it does not equal cash after all growth construction. We retain the issuer reconciliation in the dataset and show the GAAP cash-flow bridge separately, so the investor can see where each measure helps and where it stops.

The current quarter’s derivative fair-value gain was approximately $432 million, versus $574 million a year earlier, while the nine-month period carried roughly $2.1 billion of derivative losses. Those are material relative to parent earnings. Excluding non-cash marks from an operating discussion is reasonable; ignoring the actual settlement economics of the contracts is not. The relevant question is whether the eventual gas procurement and LNG sale margins deliver cash consistent with the operating thesis.

Earnings quality

NPNS reduces one volatility source; it does not create free cash

In June, Cheniere designated the normal-purchases-and-normal-sales scope exception for certain integrated production marketing agreements. The designated agreements represented approximately 73% of fixed minimum contractual IPM volumes at that date. The remaining 27%, involving upstream deliveries, was excluded. For qualifying agreements, subsequent physical purchases are no longer remeasured at fair value through earnings. Instead, purchases enter the inventory and cost-of-sales framework as gas is received and LNG is sold.

There is a second part that matters just as much. Fair values at designation became a new cost basis, with deferred gains and losses amortized over the remaining expected contract terms. That recognition depends on delivery timing and volume and is not expected to be linear. A smoother future earnings line therefore would not prove that underlying economics became less exposed to prices or execution. It would partly reflect a different accounting path. Our valuation gives no separate value to the act of changing the accounting designation.

Receivables and inventory days are screening tools, not precise forecasts for an LNG terminal. Cargo delivery dates can move a large receivable across a quarter boundary. Inventory includes materials and LNG in transit as well as natural gas. Cost-of-sales accounting can also change the denominator. We use these ratios to identify questions and then return to the filing, rather than labeling every increase as collection trouble. No current filing evidence supports calling the reported financial statements unreliable.

The scorecard’s most important caution is cash ownership. Nine-month operating cash includes the consolidated business, whereas the shareholder owns the parent equity claim. Subtracting growth capex and minority distributions leaves $1.882 billion before common buybacks and dividends of $2.166 billion. The resulting $284 million gap is not a solvency allegation; the group also has cash balances, debt financing and other investing flows. It does show that recent common returns were not fully funded by this narrower post-construction, post-minority cash measure.

Balance sheet and cash

Infrastructure assets are valuable, but the claims are substantial

At September 30, unrestricted cash was $1.663 billion and restricted cash $393 million. Current debt of $1.349 billion plus long-term debt of $22.582 billion gives $23.931 billion total debt and $22.268 billion net debt after unrestricted cash. Restricted cash is not silently treated as money freely available for repurchases. Debt, leases, construction commitments and operating liquidity all matter before cash can be distributed to common shareholders.

Property, plant and equipment of $37.469 billion dominates the asset base. The book value reflects invested capital less accounting depreciation, not a current appraisal of the terminals. Similarly, total equity of $11.970 billion includes $5.415 billion attributable to non-controlling interests; parent equity was $6.555 billion. Neither book equity nor plant book value is our valuation target. They help show the capital intensity and ownership structure that a price-to-earnings shortcut would conceal.

Annual operating cash fell from $8.418 billion in 2023 to $5.394 billion in 2024, then rose modestly to $5.539 billion in 2025. Cash capex increased across the same period, so computed FCF after those purchases fell to $2.461 billion in 2025. This history is a useful counterweight to assuming that an excellent distributable-cash year automatically becomes a permanently higher common-equity payout base. Gas markets, taxes, working capital and construction schedules all alter the conversion.

Our cash model does not punish growth expenditure as if it were wasted. Spending can produce future contracted earnings and increase terminal capacity. But the value should enter through future cash flows, not by adding the growth asset’s value while simultaneously treating every dollar spent to build it as distributable today. The same principle applies to minority ownership: an enterprise model must deduct the outside claim, while an already attributable common-cash model must not deduct it a second time.

Valuation

Three explicit routes, one deliberately rounded base case

Our base fair value is $290, with High uncertainty and 3/5 conviction. That is below the $318.94 reference price. A 15% margin of safety to base value gives a fresh-buy threshold of $246.50, provided the operating thesis remains intact. This is a price discipline, not a prediction that the shares will trade there. Existing holders may rationally tolerate the range while monitoring cash delivery; a new buyer should recognize that the current quote offers less protection against a ramp or spread disappointment.

The first route discounts common-owner cash directly. We assume $4.2 billion, $4.6 billion, $5.0 billion, $5.4 billion and $5.8 billion over five years, an 11% equity discount rate and 1.5% perpetual growth. These are analyst-normalized cash assumptions after relevant ownership and investment demands, not management guidance. The initial level sits below the $6.30 billion current DCF midpoint because that issuer measure is not cash after all growth spending. The rising path gives credit for capacity converting to cash as investment matures.

The second route makes the minority assumption explicit rather than hiding it inside a multiple. The $9.5 billion reserve is not an appraisal or quoted market value of every outside claim. It is an analyst allowance for the economic ownership that sits ahead of the common-parent claim. Raising it by $1 billion reduces per-share value by about $4.88. A reader with a different minority valuation can therefore adjust the model directly. Using the book amount without thought would make the common stock look cheaper, but book equity is not the same as a market claim on cash.

The market-implied case is demanding but not absurd. Multiplying 205.1 million model shares by $318.94, then adding net debt and the same economic NCI reserve, produces roughly $97.18 billion of enterprise claims. At the $9 billion EBITDA midpoint, that is about 10.8 times EBITDA. The premium over our enterprise route is modest; the larger caution comes from the equity cash route and the uncertainty around capital conversion. This is why the verdict is HOLD rather than an absolute rejection of the business.

Terminal assumptions remain a large part of discounted value. At a 10% discount rate, the same cash path is worth about $301 per share; at 12%, about $241. That spread is not a model defect to be concealed. It tells the investor that a narrow target price cannot carry more confidence than the long-run cash assumptions underneath it. We use scenarios and a margin of safety because the final decimals are less reliable than the operating and ownership arithmetic.

Against the Street

The ratings snapshot is heavily positive: one strong buy, 24 buys and two holds. Ratings are opinions with different horizons and methods, not an independent probability distribution. We neither count buy labels as proof of upside nor discard them because our own valuation is lower.

Management scorecard

Capital allocation is part of operating execution

Management receives credit for increasing operating capacity, delivering more cargoes and raising the full-year outlook. The most useful next assessment is whether the new fourth-quarter burden is achieved without a deterioration in cash conversion. We avoid grading the raised outlook as already delivered. The distinction keeps the scorecard forward-looking: confidence should increase when the forecast becomes a filed cash result, not merely when the range is printed.

Buybacks can add value when the repurchase price is below economic worth and the company retains adequate funding for its assets. Third-quarter repurchases were approximately 2.808 million shares for about $731 million, with monthly average prices in the mid-$250s to high-$260s. Those prices were below our current $290 base case. That historical judgment does not authorize identical enthusiasm for buybacks near the new market quote. The remaining authorization of roughly $8.382 billion is permission, not an obligation or proof of future accretion.

The common share count has fallen, which is the observable benefit of repurchases rather than just a dollar authorization headline. However, share-count reduction cannot compensate indefinitely for paying too much. Our valuation holds the denominator fixed to make the operating assumptions visible. Future accretion is upside only if the purchases are funded sensibly and occur below value. The same discipline applies to expansion: management should earn credit for attractive returns on incremental capital, not simply for a larger installed asset base.

1

Bull: contracted scale can compound

Existing infrastructure, more operating trains and lower share count can deliver a growing common-cash stream. If growth capex falls as new capacity contributes, the market may correctly look through today’s construction drag.

2

Bear: the quote capitalizes a strong moment

Marketing margins, tax timing and a back-loaded ramp can make a strong guide less repeatable than it appears. Net debt and minority claims leave common equity exposed when operating cash disappoints.

3

Our position: good assets, limited cushion

The quarter supports the operating franchise. The price asks for much of the execution benefit already. HOLD recognizes that distinction while keeping specific tests for an upgrade or downgrade.

The strongest objection to our base is that the discounted-cash model underestimates how quickly construction spending can normalize. If the group sustains the current distributable-cash level while incremental project spending falls, recurring common cash could exceed our modeled path. That would lift value without requiring a speculative multiple expansion. The bull scenario gives that outcome meaningful probability rather than treating it as impossible.

The strongest objection to the market price is that current-year DCF can look like a distributable annuity when it still finances growth assets and reflects tax or working-capital timing. The nine-month GAAP bridge supplies a concrete caution. It does not prove that the guide is wrong, nor that debt is unmanageable. It shows why investors should insist on the reconciliation between headline cash generation and cash genuinely available after the economic claims on the business. Our uncertainty rating remains High because both objections can be sensible at the same time.
