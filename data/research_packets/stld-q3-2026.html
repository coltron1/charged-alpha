Steel carries the result; aluminum must prove itself

Steel is the main earnings engine. Its operating income reached $1.033 billion. The average selling price rose to $1,400 per ton from $1,298 sequentially while the average ferrous scrap cost fell to $404 from $412. The simple price-minus-scrap indicator widened from $886 to $996 per ton. That $110 improvement is useful directional evidence, but it is not a complete gross-margin formula. Product mix, conversion costs, metallic yields, freight and timing all matter, and selling-price and scrap measures are not perfectly matched cohorts.

Fabrication generated $90.588 million of operating income. Shipments were 190,151 tons, up from 161,010 in the previous quarter, and the stated backlog was approximately 50% above the prior-year level and extended through the second quarter of 2027. That offers visibility, but backlog is not guaranteed revenue at an unchanged margin. Project schedules, cancellations, mix and the cost of steel inputs affect conversion into earnings. The strongest interpretation is that demand visibility improved; the cautious interpretation is that the realized margin still has to be earned.

Aluminum recorded $475.697 million of external revenue and a $26.226 million operating loss. The release reports roughly 55,000 metric tons of sheet shipments and 91,000 metric tons of hot-band production. Management says the coolant-related quality issue was resolved in October and does not expect ongoing costs from it. Three cold mills are operating; the first continuous annealing solution heat-treatment line is expected to produce automotive products before year-end, with the second line expected in early 2027. These are management milestones, not completed outcomes.

The operation was approximately at EBITDA breakeven, which is progress but does not equal GAAP operating profitability. Depreciation and the cost of the capital invested remain economic burdens. Our base case allows an improving operation without assuming a fully mature earnings stream immediately. The bull case needs both technical progress and commercial conversion into acceptable returns. The bear case allows further ramp costs or weaker pricing to delay that conversion. A diversified product line can reduce one form of concentration while introducing execution risk elsewhere.

Follow profit all the way to common shareholders

The gross margin was 19.7% and the operating margin was 15.5%, computed from the release. Selling, general and administrative expense was $186.974 million, profit-sharing expense $81.250 million, and intangible amortization $7.696 million. Profit sharing is a real recurring expense of the operating model and stays inside our operating-income calculation. One provider historical field omitted this distinction: its second-quarter operating income was higher than the issuer’s $700.479 million. The packet uses issuer income statements consistently across the series.

Net interest expense reduced operating income by $39.331 million, while other income added $11.548 million. Tax expense of $222.896 million implies an effective rate of 22.8%. The noncontrolling-interest adjustment added only $0.312 million to the parent result. That small current income-statement effect should not be confused with the balance-sheet claims of minority owners. We separately deduct redeemable noncontrolling interests in enterprise-value models and avoid treating negative ordinary minority equity as extra cash for common shareholders.

Adjusted EBITDA was $1.190 billion, compared with $1.184 billion of unadjusted EBITDA. The narrow adjustment gap helps this quarter’s presentation, but EBITDA still excludes interest, tax and capital consumption. It is not distributable cash and cannot be compared directly with a price per common share. Our EBITDA route first values the enterprise, then deducts net debt and the specified minority claim, and only then divides by the share denominator. The earnings route starts with common EPS and must not deduct debt a second time.

Three routes, and what the market requires

Our $190 central value is an analyst estimate, not company guidance. It sits near three explicit routes: normalized earnings, enterprise value to normalized EBITDA, and a discounted cash-flow model. The models share exposure to steel-cycle assumptions, so their agreement is not three independent confirmations. Their purpose is to expose the arithmetic and show what must be true. We do not capitalize the latest quarter as if it were permanent: $5.26 multiplied by four is $21.04, but annualizing a strong quarter can obscure cyclical reversals.

The cash-flow route models unlevered cash available to all capital providers. It is not a direct extrapolation of reported operating cash flow less capital spending, which includes financing and working-capital effects under accounting conventions. The $2.0 billion starting assumption allows a stronger normalized business than recent annual free cash flow while retaining a capital-consumption burden. Roughly 72% of enterprise value comes from the terminal calculation. That dependence is why a sensitivity grid and a wide uncertainty range are more useful than extra decimal places in a target.

At $245.17, model market equity plus net debt and redeemable minority claims implies enterprise value of $38.631 billion. Holding the 8.5× multiple constant requires normalized EBITDA of $4.545 billion. Holding the 12× earnings multiple constant requires $20.43 of sustainable EPS. In the DCF, the same price requires first-year unlevered cash flow of approximately $2.586 billion with the remaining growth and discount assumptions unchanged. These are conditional reverse calculations, not forecasts.

Those requirements are achievable in a strong cycle, especially if aluminum contributes profit and fabrication backlog converts well. Our disagreement is about how much durability to pay for before that evidence arrives. The base value implies approximately 22.5% capital downside from the reference price, excluding dividends and costs. We require a 20% discount to base value before considering a BUY, producing a $152 price threshold, and we also require operating confirmation. A falling price alone is insufficient if the underlying normalized earnings estimate is falling faster.

The strongest bull case deserves a fair hearing

The bull case is that this is a structurally better company than the historical cycle suggests. New assets can raise output, fabrication demand can cushion weaker steel markets, and aluminum can turn from a startup drag into a meaningful contributor. A strong domestic pricing environment could persist longer than our normalized model assumes. Under that combination, the Street’s higher earnings path may be reasonable and our $190 central value too cautious. The $275 bull scenario recognizes that possibility rather than pretending every favorable development is already exhausted.

The bear case is that favorable metal spreads and lower capital spending make current cash generation look more permanent than it is. A weaker steel price, renewed input inflation, slower projects or additional aluminum ramp problems could reduce earnings while the company still carries substantial invested capital and debt. Repurchases at high prices would then have used cash that might later be valuable. The $125 bear scenario is an explicit stress value, not a statistical confidence bound or an assertion that a crisis is imminent.
