The earnings print

Jabil reported fourth-quarter revenue of $10.616 billion, up 28.6% from $8.252 billion. Core operating income rose 30.1% to $675 million, while GAAP operating income increased to $602 million from $337 million. Core diluted earnings per share reached $4.40, up 33.7%; GAAP diluted earnings per share were $3.76. These measures describe the same quarter but answer different questions. Core results strip out selected expenses, whereas GAAP includes the charges actually recognized in the income statement.

The sales acceleration is unmistakable across the recent history. Revenue advanced from $8.305 billion in the first quarter to $8.282 billion in the second, $8.751 billion in the third and $10.616 billion in the fourth. That sequence does not by itself prove organic growth because acquisitions, customer mix and component pass-through can all change reported sales. The quarter also had a stronger profit conversion than a simple low-margin volume surge: core operating profit grew slightly faster than sales. Still, the rounded core margin was only 6.4%, versus 6.3% a year earlier. Small margin changes matter when the revenue base is this large.

For the fiscal year, revenue was $35.954 billion and core operating income was $2.069 billion. The fiscal-year statements in this release are preliminary and unaudited; the current annual filing had not yet appeared in the saved SEC submissions. We use the release for the new results and the already-filed annual and quarterly reports for accounting context. Later audited footnotes could refine the picture without negating the existence of today's verified earnings print.

Profit and adjustment bridge

The latest quarter's gap between GAAP and core operating income was $73 million. It comprised $24 million of amortization, $25 million of stock compensation and related charges, $9 million of restructuring, $5 million of net periodic benefit cost, and $10 million of acquisition and divestiture charges. That bridge reconciles $602 million of GAAP operating income to $675 million of core operating income. It is smaller than the prior-year quarter's $182 million bridge, which included a substantial business-divestiture loss. Accordingly, faster GAAP profit growth does not mean that recurring operating economics accelerated by the same percentage.

At the full-year level, $365 million of adjustments separate $1.704 billion of GAAP operating income from $2.069 billion of core operating income. The largest ordinary economic issue is not whether the reconciliation is disclosed; it is how much of the excluded cost should remain in a shareholder valuation. Equity compensation transfers value and can create dilution. Acquisition amortization is noncash in the current period but follows an earlier cash investment. Recurring restructuring can also be an ongoing cost of maintaining a manufacturing footprint.

Our enterprise-value route starts with management's forward core operating-margin objective, then subtracts an explicit $160 million annual equity-compensation reserve. That reserve is an analyst assumption, not company guidance or a claim that this is the only adjustment needed. The earnings-multiple route deliberately uses the familiar core EPS guide as a separate market comparison. Keeping the routes distinct makes the sensitivity visible instead of blending adjusted earnings and unadjusted cash without explanation.

Earnings quality

A strong result can coexist with an important funding dependency. The quality assessment therefore separates recognized profit, collections, inventory exposure, supplier terms, accounting adjustments and dilution. None of the figures reviewed establishes fraud or a restatement. The issue is the durability of cash conversion when several very large working-capital balances grow at once. Describing every payable increase as debt financing would also be inaccurate: ordinary trade credit is part of this business model, and the release groups multiple liabilities in the cash-flow bridge.

Receivables were $6.513 billion at year-end, inventory was $7.413 billion and accounts payable was $14.444 billion. Ending-balance proxies using a ninety-two-day quarter imply about 56 receivable days, 71 inventory days and 138 payable days. These are analytical ratios, not the company's reported cash-conversion cycle. They use ending balances rather than averages, receivables can exclude balances sold to financial institutions, and the release does not provide the purchase denominator needed for a precise payable-days calculation. Acquisitions and seasonality further limit direct comparison.

The annual and quarterly filings describe accounts-receivable sale arrangements whose proceeds enter operating cash flow. At the prior fiscal year-end, outstanding sold balances with continuing involvement included $372 million under the global securitization program and $927 million under other sale programs. The current fourth-quarter release does not refresh those footnotes. We therefore flag collection financing for follow-up without extrapolating those old balances into the new year-end. Reported cash flow is useful, but the financing arrangements behind it deserve the same attention as the earnings headline.

Who funded the expansion

Fiscal-year operating cash flow was $2.002 billion. Before working-capital movements, the cash-flow reconciliation contributes $1.864 billion. Operating assets then absorbed $7.846 billion, including receivables, contract assets, inventory, prepaid and other current assets, and other assets. The grouped accounts-payable, accrued-expense and other-liability movement supplied $7.984 billion. The net working-capital contribution was therefore only $138 million. This is the central finding: the headline cash result rests on two enormous offsetting movements, rather than on an absence of funding pressure.

That does not make the cash unreal. Supplier credit and customer-linked arrangements can be a sensible way to support a contracted manufacturing ramp. It does make the next period more sensitive to timing. If collections improve while liabilities remain stable, cash conversion could become much stronger. If customer payments slow or supplier terms tighten, the same high-growth revenue trajectory could consume cash. The size of the balances means that a relatively small change in days can matter more than another small improvement in the operating margin.

The company's adjusted free cash flow was $1.532 billion. Its calculation subtracts $628 million of property and equipment purchases from operating cash flow and adds $158 million of proceeds and advances from property and equipment sales. A simpler gross-capex measure is $1.374 billion. Both are disclosed here because the extra proceeds are a real cash source but are not interchangeable with recurring customer collections. Cash acquisitions of $852 million, treasury-stock purchases of $1.060 billion and dividends of $35 million also compete for funding outside that adjusted free-cash-flow headline. Cash ended the year $194 million lower despite stronger earnings.

Three valuation routes

Our central fair value is $300 per share, with HOLD, three-out-of-five conviction and High uncertainty. This is an analyst judgment across three independent approaches, not a price target copied from consensus. It is modestly below the pre-print reference and does not offer the twenty-percent margin of safety we would prefer for a new position facing significant cash-conversion uncertainty. A price near $240 would prompt a fresh review only if the earnings and funding thesis remained intact. A falling price caused by a broken business case would not automatically create a bargain.

The earnings route applies eighteen times management's $17.55 fiscal-year core EPS objective, yielding $315.90. That route is transparent and familiar but gives full credit to a non-GAAP forecast. The enterprise route takes $44.5 billion of guided sales at a 6.1% core operating margin, deducts our $160 million equity-compensation reserve, applies thirteen times the resulting economic operating profit, subtracts $1.64 billion of net debt, and divides by 104.787 million shares. The result is $301.26 per share. Lease obligations remain outside the funded-debt bridge and the selected operating multiple must be interpreted consistently with that treatment.

The cash-flow route starts with $1.450 billion of equity-owner cash flow after interest, below the company's $1.600 billion adjusted free-cash-flow objective. We project fifteen-percent annual growth for the next four steps, discount at ten percent and use three-percent terminal growth. The result is $290.00 per share. Because this is equity cash flow after interest, we do not subtract net debt again. The terminal value is a large share of the result, making the discount rate and long-term cash-growth assumptions consequential. A slower ten-percent growth path lowers this route to about $248 per share. These are assumptions to challenge, not observed facts.

Management and capital allocation

Management's fiscal-year 2027 plan calls for $44.5 billion of revenue, a 6.1% core operating margin, $17.55 of core EPS and approximately $1.6 billion of adjusted free cash flow. Relative to the newly reported year, that means revenue growth of 23.8%, core EPS growth of 34.1%, but adjusted free-cash-flow growth of only 4.4%. The difference is the episode's most useful forward-looking tension. It could reflect deliberate investment ahead of a larger earnings stream. It could also leave less protection if the ramp takes longer or customers change their schedules.

The first-quarter ranges provide an earlier test: revenue of $10.6 billion to $11.4 billion, core operating income of $592 million to $652 million, and core EPS of $3.80 to $4.20. These are forecasts, not booked sales. The GAAP EPS range is $2.78 to $3.18. Its $1.02 per-share reconciliation includes expected amortization, stock compensation and restructuring. The wider annual opportunity does not remove those near-term costs.

The management scorecard is therefore prospective and measurable. Delivering the revenue objective earns credit only alongside margin and cash performance. Buybacks earn credit when they improve per-share economics without weakening the balance sheet during a funding-heavy ramp. Acquisition spending earns credit when the acquired capabilities generate returns above their financing and integration cost. The annual filing's customer concentration adds another constraint: the five largest customers represented about 36% of fiscal-year 2025 revenue, and the largest anonymous customer represented 16%. We do not assign that customer a name the filing does not disclose.

Ranked risks and uncertainty

The highest-ranked risk is a mismatch between production commitments and the timing of customer cash receipts. Its potential severity is high because the receivable, inventory and payable balances are large relative to annual cash generation. Its probability is not asserted as a statistical estimate. The practical signal is whether operating assets continue absorbing cash while liability support slows. That combination would challenge the valuation even if reported sales meet guidance.

The second risk is customer concentration and program timing. Manufacturing demand can shift across product generations, and capacity built for a particular program may not be redeployed immediately. The third risk is paying too much for earnings whose cash conversion has not matured. A high-quality business can deliver its plan and still produce a disappointing investment if the starting price assumes an even stronger outcome. The fourth is acquisition integration, including whether acquired capabilities generate sufficient returns after the initial cash outlay and ongoing compensation costs.

There is a genuine upside risk to our cautious value. Contractual arrangements may make the expanded working capital less risky than a balance-sheet snapshot suggests. Collections could catch up, infrastructure demand could stay stronger for longer, and buybacks could increase each remaining share's claim on that cash. Our bull case allows that combination, but does not treat it as the default. High uncertainty here means a wide plausible range of economic outcomes, not that every unfavorable scenario is equally likely or that management's outlook lacks credibility. We will change the conclusion when the cash and operating evidence changes.
