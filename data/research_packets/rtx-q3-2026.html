Five things to know

  1. Growth extends across the portfolio. Sales reached $24.965B, with higher commercial aftermarket and defense activity. Backlog reached $340B.
  2. The earnings surge includes a recovery benefit. Adjusted EPS of $2.26 includes $0.25 from IEEPA recovery. Removing only that benefit leaves $2.01, still above last year’s $1.70.
  3. Cash timing cuts both ways. Quarterly FCF declined to $2.119B, while nine-month FCF rose to $6.306B. Contract-liability movements explain a large part of the quarterly reversal.
  4. Pratt has both momentum and customer risk. Adjusted operating profit rose to $968M, but GAAP profit fell to $608M after a $363M customer-bankruptcy charge.
  5. Valuation remains the constraint. Our three routes support $165 base value and $173.75 probability-weighted value versus $192.11 before the print. A stronger business need not be a cheap stock.

Segments and backlog

Collins sales increased to $8.382B. Commercial original equipment grew 23%, aftermarket 9% and defense 7% on the company’s organic comparison. Adjusted operating profit reached $1.788B and a 21.3% margin, including approximately $250M of IEEPA recovery. The prior-year GAAP comparison includes the actuation and flight-control business sale gain, so the reported and adjusted growth rates answer different questions. The volume improvement is valuable; the entire margin step-up is not a safe permanent run rate.

Pratt’s commercial aftermarket grew 21%, while commercial original equipment fell 5% and military rose 2%. Adjusted profit increased to $968M, including approximately $160M of IEEPA recovery. GAAP segment profit instead declined to $608M after a $363M customer-bankruptcy charge, primarily related to specific customer contract assets. A repair-driven aftermarket can generate revenue while customer credit risk still consumes economic value. We therefore monitor the profitability and collectability of that activity together.

Raytheon sales rose 14% to $8.031B and adjusted profit reached $1.134B, a 14.1% margin. The release identifies higher defense volume and improved productivity, including approximately $85M of benefits from an international-contract modification and an offset-obligation adjustment. Those benefits are different from IEEPA recovery and are not added to its disclosed EPS amount. Contract-level estimate improvements can be legitimate earnings while still requiring careful recurrence assumptions.

The $340B backlog includes $176B commercial and $164B defense, after more than $81B of new awards. Tomahawk and AMRAAM awards include multiyear undefinitized production contracts. Backlog demonstrates customer commitment, but profitability also depends on final terms, production capacity and delivery. The annual report defines backlog as remaining performance obligations on firm orders and excludes unexercised options. Its prior year-end conversion estimate is not automatically a forecast for the much larger current balance.

The profit bridge

Consolidated operating profit was $3.337B. It differs from the sum of the three segment profits because eliminations, corporate expenses, pension accounting and acquisition accounting sit outside those headline business results. The adjusted consolidated figure is $3.987B. The $650M net difference includes a $448M acquisition-accounting expense, offsetting restructuring credits, and a net collection of significant items. Treating the whole gap as cash savings would confuse accounting adjustments with spendable cash.

The Pratt customer charge and investment gain partly offset each other within the broader adjustments. The former remains an economic risk even if management considers its nature unusual; the latter should not be extrapolated simply because it increased GAAP income. A good earnings-quality assessment asks whether the contract assets are collectible and whether the business can replace temporary benefits with production and service economics. It does not equate every exclusion with misconduct.

Quarterly operating cash flow exceeds consolidated net income, but that single ratio does not settle recurrence. Receivables and contract assets together are substantial because aerospace and defense work recognizes revenue across long production and service cycles. Contract assets are not identical to overdue trade invoices. Adding them to receivables creates a useful economic exposure measure, provided the label is explicit and comparisons retain the same method.

Inventory reached $14.953B versus $13.364B at year-end. Higher output can justify more inventory, while delays or customer distress can make it harder to monetize. The cash-flow statement, rather than the balance-sheet difference alone, identifies the period’s inventory cash use because currency, portfolio and noncash movements can also change balances. The customer-bankruptcy charge makes this distinction particularly relevant at Pratt.

Stock compensation is modest relative to sales, but it is still a cost of ownership. We do not remove it again from the company’s reported cash-flow calculation and then silently change the share count. The valuation uses current diluted weighted-average shares, and the cash-flow history separately shows share-based retirement-plan matching contributions. Equity dilution, employee compensation and cash distributions are related economic questions with different accounting lines.

We do not attach a forensic fraud score to incomplete or mixed-basis inputs. The nine recurring checks are more useful here than a mechanical model whose definitions do not match long-cycle contract accounting. An improvement in headline margin should ultimately appear in common-owner earnings, collections and a stable financing requirement. That is the standard against which the next results should be judged.

Cash and the balance sheet

Quarterly free cash flow fell 47.4% to $2.119B, yet nine-month free cash flow increased 32.9% to $6.306B. Both statements are true. Last year’s third quarter benefited from a $986M contract-liability cash inflow; this quarter recorded a $980M outflow. The $1.966B swing is larger than the $1.771B decline in operating cash flow. Other lines partly offset it, so it would be wrong to describe the swing as the only cause or to ignore the stronger year-to-date position.

Three valuation routes

The valuation is intentionally explicit about recurrence. Our earnings baseline is the $7.70 full-year adjusted EPS midpoint less the disclosed $0.25 third-quarter IEEPA benefit, or $7.45. Our cash baseline is the $9.1B FCF midpoint less approximately $0.3B of related cash recovery, or $8.8B. These are analyst stress assumptions, not revised management guidance. They do not remove the customer-bankruptcy charge again because adjusted EPS already excludes that charge.

1

Earnings power

$7.45 × 24 = $178.80. The premium multiple recognizes long-cycle demand and aftermarket economics, while requiring underlying earnings to replace recovery benefits.

2

Cash multiple

$8,800M / 1,368.1M diluted shares × 26 = $167.24. A26× cash multiple is already generous; the route does not assume a distressed contractor.

3

Discounted equity cash flow

Start at $8.8B, grow8% annually for five years, discount at8.5%, then use3% perpetual growth. Present equity value $204.433B / 1,368.1M shares = $149.43.

The three routes average $165.16; rounding to the nearest five dollars gives our $165 base value. That is 14.1% below the prior close. The models are related, so averaging them does not create three independent proofs. The difference between the earnings route and discounted-cash route highlights how much of the debate rests on conversion and the price of distant growth. The terminal value contributes most of the DCF, making the discount and long-run growth assumptions consequential.

At $192.11, the same cash model requires approximately 14.1% annual cash-flow growth for five years, holding the 8.5% discount rate and 3% terminal growth constant. That is possible, particularly if program economics normalize, but it exceeds our 8% base assumption. A reverse DCF describes what the price needs under chosen parameters; it is not the market’s literal forecast and it is not an independently observed growth rate.

High uncertainty calls for a 20% margin of safety, giving a $132 entry screen. The $225 bull scenario assumes $9 of forward normalized EPS at 25×; the $140 bear assumes $7 at 20×. Neither is company guidance. Our SELL rating is a relative valuation judgment for existing exposure, not a short-sale instruction or a claim of imminent distress. Stronger repeatable cash evidence could lift the cash routes; a cheaper price could improve prospective returns without changing the business.

Capital allocation also deserves context. The 2025 annual report describes an $11B repurchase authorization initiated in October 2023, with $615M remaining at year-end 2025. That is a dated authorization balance, not an assumption of current buying power or a forecast that repurchases will resume. The same annual report identifies IAE and IAE LLC as consolidated variable-interest entities with Pratt as primary beneficiary. Their ownership economics and minority claims matter even though RTX itself is ordinary US common equity.

  1. How much of the IEEPA recovery is already reflected in the raised annual cash and EPS outlook, and what should recur?
  2. What explains the specific customer exposure and how much additional contract-asset risk remains?
  3. How does the new multiyear defense backlog convert into funded deliveries and margins?
  4. Which Pratt margin gains remain after recovery benefits and mix effects?
  5. What cash investment is needed to support the larger backlog without stretching suppliers or the balance sheet?

These are analytical questions, not invented call answers. Management’s execution language is supported by higher sales and the raised outlook, but the remaining tests are numerical. The annual audit is by PricewaterhouseCoopers, which reports serving as auditor since 1947. RTX had approximately 180,000 employees at year-end 2025, compared with 186,000 in 2024 and 185,000 in 2023. Those scale and governance facts provide context; they do not eliminate program or credit risk.

The strongest bull argument is operational: an installed engine base supports service demand, Collins participates in higher aircraft output, and Raytheon has a larger multiyear defense opportunity. If those forces lift repeatable cash flow faster than our 8% assumption, the current premium can be earned. The raised outlook and improved nine-month cash flow are evidence in favor of that case. A cautious valuation should not erase those positives.

The strongest bear argument is that investors may pay a premium for a peak rate of earnings improvement that contains recovery and contract-accounting benefits. Customer distress can also reveal risk in assets whose revenue was recognized earlier. A large backlog does not guarantee attractive final margins or prompt collections. If the company needs more inventory and capital to deliver it, revenue growth can outrun cash growth even without an accounting problem.

Government business creates both stability and concentration. The annual report places direct US-government sales at 38% of 2025 revenue, excluding foreign military sales through the government. Appropriations, export permissions, procurement priorities and contract execution can affect timing and profitability. We do not turn geopolitical headlines into guaranteed awards or extrapolate a program announcement into cash before an actual contract and delivery support it.

Our judgment lands between an operational collapse thesis and an unquestioned premium-growth thesis. The business has improved; the price still needs a demanding cash outcome. Conviction is 3/5 because outcomes are sensitive to both execution and valuation assumptions. A failure to meet the annual cash guide or another material customer charge would weaken the base case. Clean margin gains and demonstrably stronger recurring cash would challenge the SELL call.

A catalyst is useful only if it can change a decision. We will compare the annual cash outcome with the explicit remaining requirement rather than celebrate or condemn a quarter in isolation. We will also distinguish the timing of customer advances from the profitability of the underlying contracts. A collection rebound that merely restores a prior timing imbalance is valuable liquidity, but it should not automatically receive the multiple of permanent incremental earnings.

The next review should retain the same model definitions: common earnings for equity ownership, consolidated net income for the operating-cash conversion check, borrowing debt separated from leases, and diluted shares for per-share valuation. Changing those definitions in response to a favorable headline would make the track record less informative. The signpost thresholds above are analyst choices and should be updated only with an explained economic reason, not retroactively moved to turn a miss into a pass.
