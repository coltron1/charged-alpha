The print — headline growth needs a comparable bridge

Third-quarter revenue was $9.449 billion, up 5.84% from $8.928 billion a year earlier. Adjusted EBITDA was $2.067 billion versus $2.061 billion, essentially flat, while EPS excluding charges and credits fell from $0.69 to $0.64. GAAP diluted EPS was $0.49 versus $0.50. Revenue and earnings therefore tell different stories: SLB is recovering sequentially, but the annual comparison still shows pressure on the profit earned from each dollar of sales.

ChampionX is essential to interpreting the revenue increase. Its acquired businesses contributed $956 million this quarter versus $579 million in the prior-year reported period, which included two months of activity. The $377 million difference represents approximately 72% of SLB’s $521 million reported revenue increase. That arithmetic identifies the acquisition contribution; it does not attribute every remaining dollar to price or volume. Excluding the acquired businesses from both periods gives approximately 1.72% growth, consistent with the release’s rounded 2% description.

The company also supplies a different, pro forma comparison that assumes ChampionX was owned from the start of 2025. Against $9.229 billion on that basis, current revenue grew approximately 2.38%. The acquisition-excluded and pro forma calculations answer different questions and should not be mixed. The first removes acquired operations; the second includes a comparable full period. Both are substantially below headline growth, and both still show positive current progress.

The retrieved consensus record was $9.272 billion revenue and $0.617 EPS. Actuals exceeded those measures by approximately 1.91% and 3.73%. The EPS comparison uses the adjusted figure that the provider labels as actual, not GAAP EPS. A beat against that snapshot can coexist with weaker year-over-year profitability. We retain that distinction throughout the packet and avoid inventing a company revenue or EPS forecast where the release supplies none.

Segments — production grows while drilling repairs margins

Production Systems delivered $4.000 billion of revenue and $646 million of pretax operating income. Its reported revenue rose 15% year over year, but the company describes approximately 5% growth excluding the acquisition effect. The segment benefits from production chemicals, artificial lift and SLB OneSubsea activity. Those exposures can extend an existing asset’s productive life, which differs economically from relying entirely on a new drilling campaign. The distinction helps explain why the portfolio can improve even when some drilling markets remain below last year.

Well Construction generated $2.888 billion of revenue and $475 million of pretax operating income. Reservoir Performance generated $1.634 billion and $266 million, respectively. Both recovered sequentially as Middle East activity improved, but both still reported roughly 3% lower year-over-year revenue and about 15% lower operating income. Their margins were 16.4% and 16.3%, below the prior-year levels. A rebound from a disrupted quarter is useful evidence, but it does not establish that prior margins have already returned.

Digital revenue was $693 million with $194 million of pretax operating income, a 28.0% margin. Revenue declined slightly sequentially as lower exploration-data sales offset improvement elsewhere. Platforms and Applications grew sequentially to $299 million and Digital Operations reached $163 million. These recurring and workflow-oriented activities deserve attention, but Digital also contains more variable exploration and services revenue. Treating the entire division as subscription software would overstate its recurring character.

Digital annualized recurring revenue was reported at $1.04 billion, with 13% growth stated by the issuer. Dividing the rounded $1.04 billion by the rounded $926 million comparator produces about 12.3%; that rounding difference is disclosed rather than silently changing the company’s reported growth rate. Data Center Solutions revenue grew 23% sequentially and 76% year over year, but a separate absolute revenue and profit figure is not supplied here. We do not assign the entire All Other segment to data centers or invent a standalone margin.

Profit bridge — improved activity does not erase the charges

The distinction between adjusted and GAAP income is visible, not cosmetic. SLB reported $735 million of attributable GAAP net income and $962 million excluding charges and credits. The $227 million difference consists of a $187 million pension settlement charge and $40 million after-tax, after-noncontrolling-interest merger and integration costs. The pension settlement is noncash in the current operating-cash reconciliation. That supports treating it differently from routine operating expenditure, while still recognizing that pension commitments are real obligations.

Pretax segment operating income of $1.576 billion is not the same as consolidated pretax earnings. Corporate and other costs subtract $204 million, corporate interest income adds $22 million, interest expense subtracts $133 million and charges and credits subtract $242 million. The resulting $1.019 billion pretax income is followed by $239 million tax and $45 million attributable to noncontrolling interests, leaving $735 million for SLB. This bridge prevents the most profitable segment measure from being mistaken for common-shareholder earnings.

Adjusted EBITDA margin was 21.88%, approximately 121 basis points below the prior-year 23.08% calculated from reported dollars. Sequentially, the margin improved from the release’s rounded 21.2% to 21.9%. Both statements are true. The improvement supports the recovery thesis, while the annual decline shows that the business is still absorbing a weaker operating environment and changes in mix. Neither comparison should be selected alone merely because it makes a stronger headline.

Tax also affects the apparent earnings gap. The GAAP effective rate was approximately 23.45%, compared with about 19.98% excluding charges and credits. Pension and acquisition accounting influence the denominator and tax treatment. Applying the adjusted rate mechanically to every future charge would be unjustified. Our valuation uses explicitly normalized assumptions and tests them against cash, rather than declaring all excluded costs permanently irrelevant.

Earnings quality — cash conversion has a second hurdle

The same nine checks are applied each quarter. Stock compensation was $72 million, or approximately 0.76% of revenue, a relatively modest fraction of the sales base. The adjusted-to-GAAP net-income difference was $227 million, approximately 30.9% of GAAP attributable earnings, making reconciliation material. Below-the-line items include $68 million of interest and other income, of which $25 million was interest income and $43 million earnings from equity-method investments. Those sources should not be described as service revenue.

Noncontrolling interests absorbed $45 million, approximately 5.8% of consolidated net income. SLB owns 70% of OneSubsea, so consolidated operations can create profits that do not all belong to SLB shareholders. The quarter’s entire minority allocation is not assigned to that joint venture without disclosure. The valuation denominator and equity bridge are kept transparent to avoid assuming every consolidated dollar belongs to the common stock.

Nine-month operating cash flow was $3.356 billion, exceeding $2.356 billion consolidated net income. That first conversion test is constructive. After capital investment, however, issuer free cash flow was $1.521 billion, about 66.9% of attributable net income and below the $1.826 billion prior-year figure. Cash must fund equipment, asset-performance investments and exploration data before it can support distributions. EBITDA and operating cash alone do not measure that residual funding capacity.

Ending receivable and inventory proxies were approximately 93.8 and 63.7 days, calculated with a 91-day quarter. These are ending-balance monitoring ratios, not average-balance collection or inventory-cycle measurements. They can be affected by geography, billing milestones, acquisitions and seasonality. The useful question is whether the next filing explains deterioration alongside cash conversion. The ninth check, guidance delivery, remains open for the unchanged approximately $2.5 billion capital-investment plan; we do not fabricate a numerical revenue promise to grade management against.

Cash and capital — distributions run ahead of free cash

The three investments total $682 million, deducted from $1.510 billion operating cash flow. During the same quarter, repurchases used $656 million and dividends used $439 million. Distributions therefore exceeded free cash flow by $267 million. The company can fund a temporary difference, but the difference must remain visible when assessing the generosity of its capital-return policy.

The nine-month picture is more demanding. Repurchases of $1.755 billion and dividends of $1.305 billion total $3.060 billion, approximately twice the $1.521 billion free cash generated. The $1.539 billion shortfall is a funding comparison, not a claim that every dollar was borrowed specifically for buybacks. Acquisitions, employee stock proceeds, currency movements and other items also affect debt. The issuer’s complete liquidity bridge shows net debt rising $1.825 billion from year-end to $9.249 billion.

Cash and short-term investments were $3.551 billion against $12.800 billion gross debt. It remains an ordinary acquisition of a continuing listed parent, so it does not prevent coverage; it does create an important future funding and integration test.

Capital-investment guidance remains approximately $2.5 billion for 2026. Nine-month investment under that same definition totals $1.835 billion, leaving approximately $665 million if the annual plan is met. This is computed remaining capacity, not a separately issued fourth-quarter forecast. Historical cash is seasonal: the fourth quarter of 2025 generated $2.289 billion of free cash flow. A strong finish could improve the annual funding balance, but repeating that historical amount is an assumption that investors should test rather than quietly bake into a bullish conclusion.

The enterprise-value route assumes $8.5 billion of normalized EBITDA at ten times, subtracts $9.249 billion current net debt, then divides by 1.497 billion diluted weighted-average shares. It produces $50.60 per share. That route uses an explicit share denominator rather than silently switching between period-end shares and the provider’s market capitalization. It is a simplified debt-and-cash bridge; operating leases and minority interests are not separately capitalized, so it should not be compared uncritically with an enterprise multiple that includes those claims.

The cash route models annual enterprise cash flows of $4.3 billion, $4.8 billion, $5.2 billion, $5.5 billion and $5.8 billion, discounted at 9% with 2.5% terminal growth. Risks — the bull case must finance its own growth

The leading operating risk is that the sequential recovery stalls before margins regain their prior-year level. Middle East conflict, project disruption and customer budget changes can affect both activity and collection. Offshore awards improve visibility but do not eliminate execution costs or the timing between booking, delivery and cash. We assign this risk High potential impact, while avoiding a fabricated probability for a specific geopolitical outcome.

The second risk is capital-allocation congestion. Buybacks, dividends, ordinary reinvestment and Kelvion all make claims on cash. Each can be reasonable on its own; together they can leave less balance-sheet room if the recovery disappoints. The current net-debt increase is not proof of distress, but it makes the next cash bridge consequential. A business can report positive earnings and still reduce financial flexibility through the timing of its investments and distributions.

Third is paying for a data-center narrative ahead of disclosed economics. The 76% year-over-year revenue increase is strong, and thermal management could broaden the offering. Yet the release does not provide a complete standalone Data Center Solutions income statement. We cannot infer high software-like margins or guaranteed synergies from its growth rate. Integration, manufacturing execution, customer concentration within the new activity and the acquisition price all remain important questions.

The strongest bull case combines renewed Middle East activity, offshore backlog conversion, production optimization and expanding recurring digital workflows. That combination can raise earnings while smoothing some drilling cyclicality. The strongest bear case is that acquired revenue masks weak underlying growth, margins remain below historical levels, and cash obligations expand before returns arrive. Our $35 bear, $50 base and $74 bull cases make that range visible. Probabilities of 25%, 50% and 25% are our assumptions, not frequencies derived from a statistical model.
