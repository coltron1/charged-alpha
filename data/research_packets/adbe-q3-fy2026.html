Customers and reporting

The customer groups are not standalone profit segments.

Adobe changed its formal reporting structure in the first quarter of fiscal 2026. Digital Media, Digital Experience and Publishing and Advertising were combined into a single operating and reportable segment as management shifted toward unified selling and integrated product development. The latest available 10-Q explains that the CEO evaluates consolidated net income and resources. Customer-group subscription revenue is useful supplemental information, but it is not a disclosure of two independently measured operating profits. Allocating corporate profit to those groups by revenue share would create numbers the company has not reported.

Creative & Marketing Professionals subscription revenue reaches approximately $4.65B, up a reported 13%. Business Professionals & Consumers reaches $1.91B, up 16%. The former includes Experience Cloud and Creative Cloud flagship applications; the latter includes Acrobat and Express. The June investor datasheet backfills both groups across earlier quarters, enabling a meaningful revenue comparison despite the changed financial presentation. Its values are rounded to hundredths of a billion, so totals can differ from the sum of the displayed components. Those rounding differences are retained rather than forced to reconcile by altering a group.

The segment change improves alignment with management’s customer strategy but reduces the usefulness of old segment-margin narratives. Historical Digital Media gross profit and Digital Experience gross profit remain available in the saved filings; they should be labeled historical and are not carried forward as current group profitability. The current release does not independently reveal Firefly profit, the profitability of free Express users, or the cost of serving a particular AI cohort. Missing disclosure is part of the analytical boundary, not a license to infer a favorable margin from a product label.

Semrush further changes the comparison. Adobe completed the acquisition on April 28 for approximately $1.87B, principally cash. Q2 included about $40M customer-group subscription revenue and $480M ARR from the acquisition, while cash paid net of acquired cash was $1.56B in the first half. The Q3 release does not separately disclose the acquired revenue contribution. Consolidated growth therefore cannot be relabeled organic growth. The strategic fit with search and brand visibility may be useful, but the investment earns a return only when incremental cash compensates for the purchase price and integration costs.

Profit bridge

Growth has a cost, even before the non-GAAP adjustment.

Gross profit rises $651M to $5,997M, but cost of revenue grows 18.8%, faster than sales. The calculated gross margin slips roughly 57 basis points to 88.7%. Research and development increases 18.4% to $1,288M, sales and marketing increases 11.5% to $1,827M, and general and administrative costs increase 19.6% to $488M. Operating-expense amortization adds $40M. These are the disclosed cost lines; the release does not provide a clean incremental AI inference-cost bridge. Assigning the whole margin movement to generative AI would overstate what the available evidence establishes.

GAAP operating margin is approximately 34.8%, down about 147 basis points year over year. The adjusted margin is approximately 44.0%, down about 232 basis points. Both views therefore show a margin constraint rather than a discrepancy caused only by one accounting charge. Management still targets roughly 45% adjusted operating margin for the full year and roughly 44% for Q4. Those ranges support substantial profitability, but they make it difficult to justify a valuation that requires an immediate return to the older, higher adjusted-margin run rate.

Below operations, interest expense is $66M, investment gains are $21M and other income is $48M. Their net contribution is only $3M. Pretax income is $2,357M and tax expense is $530M, an effective rate of approximately 22.5%. The prior-year tax provision was $415M on $2,187M pretax income. A higher tax burden helps explain why net income growth is slower than operating growth. This is an important distinction for an investor looking at EPS: neither the lower share count nor a favorable adjusted tax convention turns tax expense into a nonexistent shareholder cost.

Earnings quality

Compensation is the largest recurring reconciliation item.

The $620M gap between GAAP and adjusted operating income includes $544M stock-based and deferred compensation, $58M amortization of intangibles and $18M acquisition-related expense. Compensation accounts for 87.7% of the operating adjustment. The cash-flow statement’s SBC add-back is $539M, slightly different from the broader non-GAAP compensation category. They are not interchangeable labels. Our owner-cash proxy deducts the actual cash-flow SBC add-back from free cash flow; the non-GAAP bridge preserves management’s separate $544M line.

Current net income of $1,827M reconciles to $2,424M adjusted net income after the operating additions, removal of $21M investment gains and a $2M income-tax adjustment. Adjusted EPS therefore answers a different question from the amount earned under GAAP. Adobe can legitimately use that measure to manage operations, but owners still surrender value through equity compensation or the cash needed to offset its dilution. Deducting SBC from free cash is an analytical way of acknowledging that cost; it does not claim employees were paid the same amount in cash this quarter.

The nine-quarter history also contains genuine unusual items. Fiscal 2024 includes the $1B Figma termination payment, while Q2 fiscal 2026 includes a $70M Publishing & Advertising goodwill impairment. Earlier fiscal 2026 loss-contingency charges also enter the reconciliation. These items should be explained, not treated as interchangeable with recurring stock awards. The abandoned Figma transaction is resolved for eligibility because a primary December 2023 filing documents termination, yet its economic cost remains relevant when judging acquisition discipline. A failed deal can be over without its financial lesson becoming irrelevant.

The balance of quality evidence is positive but qualified. Operating cash exceeds net income this quarter and over the trailing year. Revenue remains overwhelmingly subscription-based. Against that, current gross and operating margins are lower, tax rates have increased, and the company continues to make substantial compensation adjustments. The quality score is therefore not a binary rejection of non-GAAP reporting. It is a requirement that the valuation charge recurring costs, retain taxes and distinguish the repeatable cash engine from transactions that should not recur every year.

Cash and capital

The franchise generates cash; distribution discipline still matters.

Quarterly operating cash flow is $2,523M, versus $2,198M a year earlier. Capital spending of $85M leaves $2,438M free cash flow, up 14.7%. Deducting $539M SBC produces a $1,899M owner-cash proxy. The business converts earnings into cash well, and the small capital-expenditure line reflects a software model rather than a retailer’s physical expansion. However, the operating cash statement also includes deferred-revenue movements and other working-capital changes. A single quarter should not be annualized without considering collections and contract timing.

The three audited fiscal years provide a stronger base. Free cash flow increased from $6,942M in fiscal 2023 to $7,873M in fiscal 2024 and $9,852M in fiscal 2025. After the SBC charge, those amounts are $5,224M, $6,040M and $7,910M. The latest trailing four quarters produce $10,592M FCF and $8,521M after SBC. This is a substantial cash franchise. The valuation question is whether that owner cash can grow as AI products expand usage while prices, distribution and serving costs change.

Repurchases are not automatically value creation. Adobe paid $2,232M for stock this quarter, compared with $1,899M of free cash after our compensation charge. The cash purchase exceeds that proxy by $333M. Management describes approximately 9.5M shares repurchased, implying a rough $234.95 average cash price. That is below our $300 appraisal, which supports the economic case for the purchases, but the share number is rounded and settlement timing can differ from cash-flow presentation. The estimate is a diagnostic, not an execution-price audit.

Quarter-end cash of $4,359M plus short-term investments of $1,280M totals $5,639M. Current and long-term debt carrying amounts total $6,363M, leaving $724M net debt on that accounting basis. The current earnings release does not include the complete principal, swap and discount reconciliation. The Q2 10-Q reported $6.15B senior notes and $500M commercial paper; current-quarter financing cash includes $250M debt repayment. We use current carrying debt explicitly and flag the missing par bridge. We do not silently reconstruct it from incomplete movements.

Ordinary lease expense remains inside operating earnings and cash flows, so the enterprise models do not subtract the full $399M lease liability a second time. Semrush’s completed purchase is already reflected in cash, and no future repurchase is assumed in the model denominator. The release’s 395M diluted weighted-average shares are an analyst valuation convention, not an exact current common-share count. The latest available filing-cover count is 397.5M at June 11; Q4 guidance assumes approximately 389M diluted shares. Showing all three prevents false precision and the temptation to count a future share reduction without paying for it.

Valuation

Three routes; one explicit set of costs and uncertainty.

The fair-value anchor is $300. It comes from a $299.28 blend of three methods rather than from an old share-price high or a consensus target. The routes deliberately disagree: the direct earnings and operating-profit approaches are above $300, while the discounted-cash-flow result is approximately $263. That lower cash result is useful discipline. It shows that even a business with substantial reported cash does not deserve an unlimited multiple when future growth, competition and recurring compensation remain uncertain.

Route one applies 17 times management’s fiscal-year GAAP EPS midpoint of $18.145, producing $308.47. This route retains stock compensation and the other charges embedded in the annual GAAP outlook rather than silently normalizing them away. The multiple assumes the installed base and recurring contracts retain economic value while growth moderates. Seventeen times is an analyst judgment, not a company target or an assertion that Adobe must regain its historical premium. Because this is an equity earnings multiple, no additional cash is added to the resulting per-share value.

Route two assumes $27.5B revenue over the coming twelve months and a 35% normalized GAAP operating margin, giving $9,625M operating income. This is a forward model, distinct from current reported revenue and from the $27.5B ARR measure that happens to have the same numerical magnitude. At thirteen times operating income, enterprise value is $125,125M. Subtract $724M net debt and divide by the fixed 395M diluted-share convention to obtain $314.94 per share. The 35% margin includes recurring compensation and normal rent. We do not apply an adjusted-profit multiple while forgetting the largest recurring adjustment.

Route three begins with $8.2B unlevered owner cash after recurring compensation and ordinary reinvestment. This is an explicit forward assumption, not a relabeling of reported operating cash. It is below the observed $8.521B trailing levered FCF-less-SBC proxy, even before considering an interest add-back, providing a conservative starting point without inventing a precise conversion. The model grows that cash 5% annually for five years, discounts at 11% and uses 2.5% perpetual growth. It assumes continuing relevance and modest expansion, not domination of every AI application market.

The five cash flows are $8.200B, $8.610B, $9.0405B, $9.4925B and $9.9672B. Their present values sum to approximately $33.154B. The discounted terminal value is approximately $71.328B, giving $104.482B enterprise value. Net debt reduces equity value to $103.758B; divided by 395M model shares, the result is $262.68. Terminal value remains a large share of the answer, which is why the sensitivity grid and uncertainty classification matter. A well-formatted model is not precise merely because it produces two decimals.

AI and the outlook

Reach, contracted revenue and cash monetization are different measures.

Adobe highlights more than 150% year-over-year growth in AI-first ARR and a milestone of one billion monthly active users across creativity and productivity solutions. Both can be evidence of adoption. Neither tells us how many users pay, what each pays, or the cash margin on the incremental workload. The current release does not state an absolute AI-first ARR level, while Q2 had disclosed more than $500M. It would be wrong to manufacture the current dollar amount by multiplying the earlier lower bound by the new growth rate, which compares a different prior-year quarter.

Total Adobe ARR is $27.50B, but even this familiar metric requires a definition check. Adobe measures annual contract value using currency rates set in December and revalues the previous fiscal year for the next measurement year. The June datasheet shows prior Q3 at $24.74B using December 2025 rates, versus an earlier reported $24.28B using December 2024 rates. Comparing $27.50B with the same-FX $24.74B gives 11.16% growth. The annual reset also changes FY2025 ending ARR from $25.20B to $25.66B. That $460M is currency revaluation, not newly signed contracts.

RPO reaches $22.16B, up 8.4% year over year but slightly below $22.27B in Q2. The company says 67% is current RPO. The datasheet defines that percentage with an exclusion for certain non-cancellable and nonrefundable enterprise committed funds, so it should not be casually multiplied into a precise next-year revenue guarantee. Contract timing can explain sequential variation, but persistent RPO growth materially below subscription sales would warrant closer attention. We want adoption, bookings and cash to tell a coherent story over several quarters.

Leadership will also change. Anil Chakravarthy becomes president and CEO on December 1, while Shantanu Narayen becomes executive chair. The current September release still correctly identifies Narayen as CEO and Steve Day as interim CFO. David Wadhwani is due to step down from his creativity and productivity leadership role on September 27 and serve as a senior advisor during transition. These are meaningful operating changes, not automatic evidence of failure or success. The next team will need to demonstrate how broader usage becomes durable paid demand without sacrificing the margin and cash characteristics that make Adobe valuable.

Competing cases

A valuable installed base can coexist with disruption risk.

The strongest bullish case begins with the existing cash engine. Subscription revenue is growing at a double-digit rate, contracts support visibility, and the company continues to convert income into cash while reducing the share count. AI may expand the number of people who can create useful content and increase the value of integrated workflows. Acrobat, Express, flagship creative applications and enterprise marketing tools can reinforce distribution. If that reach improves paid conversion and product utility, the current earnings multiple could understate the durability of the franchise.

The bearish case does not require Adobe to disappear. It requires only that competitive tools weaken pricing, change workflow ownership or increase the cost of keeping customers engaged. A large free audience may be expensive to serve without paying proportionately. Enterprise customers can demand concessions, and a slower contract-growth rate can eventually reach recognized revenue. Margin contraction already exists in both reported and adjusted results, although its precise causes are not separately quantified. A valuation based on yesterday’s profitability would be vulnerable if these changes prove structural rather than transitional.

Capital allocation can amplify either outcome. Repurchases below value improve per-share ownership; repurchases that offset employee issuance or absorb cash before weaker results do less for outside owners. Acquisitions can strengthen capabilities, but the Figma termination payment and current goodwill/contingency charges show why capital decisions deserve independent scrutiny. Semrush is a completed transaction whose future return remains to be demonstrated. We credit integration opportunities without assuming that every acquired dollar of ARR produces the same return as organic subscription expansion.

Our HOLD reflects the balance of these arguments. The business is profitable enough and the cash record strong enough that a categorical bearish conclusion would ignore substantial evidence. The uncertainty around AI monetization, margin durability, leadership and share count is also too meaningful to waive the margin-of-safety threshold merely because the stock is well below a former high. The model can be wrong in both directions. The response is an explicit price discipline and dated operating tests, rather than a confident story that explains every available number after the fact.
