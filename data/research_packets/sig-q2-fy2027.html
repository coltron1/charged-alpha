A large earnings-day jump, followed by a lower live quote.

The print

More profit, slightly less sales, and a different comparable-sales perimeter.

Second-quarter sales were $1,528.1 million, down 0.5% from $1,535.1 million. Adjusted diluted EPS was $2.19, versus $1.61 a year earlier; GAAP diluted EPS was $1.33, versus a $0.22 loss. The FMP earnings record reports the adjusted $2.19 outcome against a $1.69 estimate. Revenue was almost exactly in line with its $1,529.53 million estimate. Mixing GAAP EPS with that adjusted estimate would create a false miss, while calling the revenue result a major beat would overstate the evidence.

Same-store sales increased 2.2%, but Blue Nile and James Allen were removed from that calculation beginning this quarter because of their transition. This qualification belongs beside the growth rate. A comparable-store measure is useful for judging the retained estate, yet it is not a measure of total corporate growth. Non-comparable sales and currency left consolidated revenue slightly lower. The historical comp series is preserved as originally reported; subsequent releases can recast the comparison perimeter. We do not manufacture a constant-perimeter nine-quarter series from insufficient disclosure.

Average unit retail increased approximately 6%. That observation does not by itself establish strong unit demand across the whole business. Management described better performance at higher price points, while the portfolio transition and commodity environment complicate any simple price-times-volume inference. This is why our model asks for retained profit and cash, rather than assuming that a positive comp automatically deserves a higher valuation multiple. The next quarterly report should show whether the new comparable base continues to improve after the initial merchandising and marketing changes.

Segments

North America pays the bills; the smaller businesses do not yet earn a premium.

North America produced $1,428.3 million of sales and $123.0 million of adjusted operating income. Its scale makes it the main source of both upside and risk. International sales were $96.6 million, with an adjusted operating loss of $1.4 million. The diamond-sourcing Other segment contributed $3.2 million of sales and a $0.5 million loss; corporate and unallocated adjusted costs were $13.9 million. These components reconcile to $107.2 million of consolidated adjusted operating income. A stronger small segment cannot offset a substantial reversal in the North American business.

The store base declined to 2,534 locations from 2,623 a year earlier. Closures can improve returns when weaker locations are removed, but they also make total sales less responsive to positive comparable-store growth. The distinction is important for interpreting the company’s branding strategy: operational concentration can be sensible even when the top line does not expand. Our valuation does not require new-store expansion. It does require that retained stores and digital brands produce enough contribution profit to pay central costs without repeatedly sacrificing gross profit.

The digital transition is not costless. James Allen’s expected full-year revenue reduction is $60–$80 million, with minimal expected adjusted operating-income impact according to management. That is an operating forecast, not proof that past acquisition spending was fully productive. The current impairment charges and the diminished James Allen sales base remind shareholders to assess both the cash originally invested and the future earnings being retained. We would prefer transparent brand-level contribution and cash returns; those are not disclosed consistently enough to construct a nine-quarter brand-profit table. The packet therefore uses the reported operating segments.

Profit bridge

The refund matters before the adjustment labels even begin.

Adjusted operating income increased $21.8 million. The quarter included approximately $15 million of refunds for tariffs paid previously, $13 million above management’s prior expectation. In a simple counterfactual that removes the full disclosed refund, adjusted operating income is $92.2 million, still up 8.0%. The refund accounts for 68.8% of the reported dollar improvement under that comparison. This is our analytical normalization, not a company-reported non-GAAP metric. It assumes the disclosed refund flowed through gross profit and does not attempt to estimate offsetting tariffs embedded elsewhere in product costs.

The gross-profit comparison is more restrained than the reported margin suggests. Reported gross profit grew $10.5 million, less than the $15 million refund. Removing that refund gives $587.4 million and a 38.44% margin, approximately 12 basis points below the prior-year margin. Lower inventory and distribution expenses helped, and higher gold costs offset part of the benefit. SG&A fell to $493.6 million from $505.3 million. Cost discipline is thus visible even after the refund adjustment. The evidence supports improvement, but it is a narrower improvement than the headline operating-income growth suggests.

From adjusted operating income of $107.2 million, the operating reconciliation removes $19.3 million of impairment add-backs and $0.4 million of restructuring adjustments to reach $87.5 million GAAP operating income. Net interest income adds $2.3 million; other non-operating expense subtracts $16.9 million; taxes subtract $20.8 million, leaving $52.1 million of net income. The non-operating line includes the impact of an equity-method investment and loans-receivable impairment. Its $19.2 million charge is a distinct item from the operating impairments. Treating the two as one cost would obscure the route from store profit to shareholder earnings.

Annual results add a further comparability warning. Fiscal 2024 net income of $810.4 million included a $170.6 million income-tax benefit, so that year is not a clean earnings-power anchor. Fiscal 2025 net income of $61.2 million became a $35.6 million loss attributable to common shareholders after preferred-share distributions. Those preferred shares were subsequently fully redeemed. Neither the unusual tax benefit nor the old preferred burden should be projected mechanically into current common earnings. The audited annual statements are retained alongside the quarterly bridge so readers can see this change in who received the reported profit.

Earnings quality

A useful cash generator with several adjustment and comparison traps.

Our nine-check scorecard is deliberately mixed. Share compensation is small relative to sales, and no current minority or preferred distribution reduces common income. But the gap between adjusted and GAAP performance remains material, the profit uplift contains refunds, and the quarter’s free-cash-flow conversion weakened. A favorable audit opinion is not a guarantee of economic quality; equally, an impairment is not proof that the current operations cannot generate cash. These checks distinguish the accounting presentation, the cash realization, and the assumptions used for valuation.

Inventory fell 1.4% year over year to $1,959.4 million, while revenue fell 0.5%. That is encouraging, but dollar inventory can be affected by gold prices, mix and valuation allowances. Our days estimate uses quarter-end inventory divided by quarterly cost of sales times 91 days. It is a reproducible point-in-time comparison, not a claim about average inventory or cash conversion across the year. Signet does not separately present accounts receivable in these recent releases, and it has not retained in-house customer finance receivables since fiscal 2022. We leave receivable days unavailable rather than relabeling other current assets.

The stronger annual cash record is the main counterweight to the adjustments. Audited free cash flow rose from $421.4 million in fiscal 2024 to $437.9 million in fiscal 2025 and $525.3 million in fiscal 2026. Subtracting share compensation gives an owner-cash proxy, but this is not a company metric and it does not reserve every future restructuring cost or acquisition payment. We use it as a check on whether accounting earnings eventually become distributable resources. The trailing result supports a cash-generation thesis; it does not erase the requirement to rebuild inventories and fund rent before the holiday season.

Balance sheet

No funded debt does not make all cash surplus.

Cash at August 1 was $526.8 million, versus $874.8 million at fiscal year-end and $281.4 million a year earlier. The first-half cash reduction reflects seasonal operating outflows, capital spending, and shareholder distributions. First-half free cash flow was negative $138.4 million, while common-share repurchases and dividends used $196.7 million. This is not evidence of immediate financial distress: the business historically earns a large portion of cash in the fourth quarter. It is evidence that the calendar matters when describing the cash balance as excess capital.

We deduct the disclosed $33 million of completed post-quarter repurchases from quarter-end cash for a simplified pro forma balance of $493.8 million. We then reserve $250 million for operating flexibility and seasonality, leaving $243.8 million credited as excess cash in enterprise-value models. The reserve is our valuation assumption, not restricted cash and not management guidance. We do not reduce shares or cash for the intended $125 million accelerated repurchase before it is completed. Counting a future lower share count while leaving all the repurchase cash on the balance sheet would create value by spreadsheet rather than by economics.

The share-count source also matters. The latest 10-Q cover reports 38,317,243 common shares outstanding on September 4. The saved FMP float feed reports 39.3298 million, while management’s annual forecast uses 38.8 million weighted diluted shares. These are three different observations, with different dates or definitions. Our market capitalization and enterprise-value routes use the primary cover count. Our forward-EPS normalization uses management’s weighted diluted forecast. The two denominators are explicitly identified; the packet does not silently substitute the larger vendor count.

Operating lease liabilities total $1,224.4 million. Our earnings and cash-flow routes already include normal store rent in their operating economics, so we do not subtract the lease liability again as if it were funded borrowing. A lease-adjusted enterprise-value comparison would require a matching pre-rent earnings measure and a consistent peer framework. The economic obligation is nevertheless real: weak sales cannot instantly remove contractual occupancy costs. The reserve and relatively restrained earnings multiple partly reflect that inflexibility.

Valuation

Three routes converge near $105, with little room for error at the quote.

The model distinguishes observable inputs from our choices. We start with the $570 million midpoint of management’s adjusted operating-income guide. We remove $30 million of annual tariff refunds and retain only half of the $35 million midpoint contribution from the renewed credit agreement. That gives $522.5 million of normalized adjusted operating income. The partnership may be durable, and the signing bonus is recognized over the agreement term rather than immediately. Our partial haircut reflects uncertainty about the split between recurring profit share, amortized bonus, ramp timing and future economics. It is not an allegation that the disclosed revenue is improper.

3

Discounted cash flow: $104.05

Start with $350M annual unlevered owner-cash flow after ordinary rent, capex, tax and an economic allowance for share compensation. Grow 2% for five forecast years; discount at 11%; terminal growth 1.5%. Operating present value $3,742.95M plus $243.8M excess cash, divided by 38.317243M shares = $104.05.

The earnings route receives 40% weight, the operating-profit route 35%, and DCF 25%. Their calculated blend is $103.44, rounded to a practical $105 anchor rather than presented with false precision. These routes are cross-checks, not statistically independent estimates: all ultimately rely on the same stores, customers and cost structure. The DCF’s $350 million starting flow is deliberately below trailing free cash flow less share compensation of $505.8 million. It allows for a less favorable working-capital cycle, investment requirements and costs excluded from adjusted earnings. The reserve is separate from recurring cash-flow generation.

At $98.11, market capitalization using primary common shares is $3,759.3 million. Subtracting modeled excess cash leaves $3,515.5 million paid for operations, or 6.73 times normalized operating income. Under the same DCF discount and growth assumptions, the price requires approximately $328.7 million of initial annual cash generation. Our case assumes $350 million. That is a cushion, but it is not so large that holiday execution, financing economics and consumer demand can be ignored.

The 9.5-times earnings and 7.5-times operating multiples are analyst judgments. We do not call them the sector’s observed median. They reflect a mature discretionary retailer with shrinking selling space, cyclical demand, repeated restructuring and real cash generation. Stronger evidence of recurring growth could support a higher multiple; a reversal in gross margin or credit economics could justify less. High uncertainty means we seek a 25% discount to base value before initiating a valuation-driven BUY, around $78.75 under this model, alongside an intact thesis. A lower price caused by permanent earnings damage would require a fresh model.

Management

Credit improvements deserve credit; they still need transparent measurement.

The renewed Bread Financial partnership runs through December 31, 2035, with profit sharing and a signing bonus recognized over the agreement’s life. The 8-K says the bonus may be repayable in certain termination circumstances. It also preserves an option to purchase or arrange a third-party purchase of programme assets when the agreement ends. That option is not current ownership of the receivables. The disclosed agreement summary supports an enduring commercial relationship, but exact bonus amounts and profit-share economics are not provided. The full agreement is expected with the next quarterly report, so no undisclosed contract margin is inserted into our model.

Management raised the annual operating-income midpoint by $50 million and adjusted EPS midpoint by 11.9%, while keeping the sales range unchanged. The credit contribution, refunds and share repurchases explain why profit can rise without a corresponding revenue-range increase. This is economically plausible. Our reservation concerns durability and price paid for that improvement, not the legitimacy of managing the balance sheet. The announced buyback capacity is authorization; it is not a promise to spend every dollar, and an intended accelerated programme is not yet an executed reduction of shares.

Risks

The bear case is weaker repeatability; the bull case is ordinary cash compounding.

The strongest bull argument is that the market still prices Signet as a structurally declining retailer even though it has removed funded debt, tightened costs and generated substantial annual cash. The credit partnership can create recurring economics, closures can improve the average store, and repurchases below intrinsic value can raise per-share results. Our model deliberately does not require a boom in store openings or a return to the unusually favorable fiscal 2024 tax result. If the retained portfolio compounds modestly and the partnership cash is durable, the bull range is plausible.

The strongest bear argument is that headline EPS growth combines refunds, a revised comp perimeter, non-GAAP exclusions and share-count effects while underlying customer demand remains vulnerable. Gold inflation, weaker discretionary spending or less favorable financing economics could leave the company paying fixed rent and marketing expenses on insufficient gross profit. Buybacks would then have consumed cash ahead of a weaker season. The absence of funded debt reduces one risk, but does not turn inventory into cash instantly or make future earnings non-cyclical.

Our HOLD reflects this tension. The three-route value is modestly above the saved quote, yet the range of outcomes is broad enough that a small discount is not decisive. Conviction is three out of five: there is a coherent cash-supported business and a reproducible model, but less confidence in the precise sustainable contribution of new contract economics and the current merchandise margin. Scenario probabilities are our judgments, not calibrated frequencies. They should change if the signposts show that ordinary operating performance is better or worse than the assumptions used here.

Catalysts

Grade the economics when the next report supplies the missing detail.

The next quarterly report is the principal checkpoint, rather than a promised share-price catalyst. We use December 2026 as the expected review window; the exact earnings date is not independently confirmed here. That report should provide third-quarter operating performance, the initial credit-agreement contribution, updated holiday expectations and details of capital returns. A positive result would be more convincing if profit improves without another disproportionate refund benefit and if the company provides a clearer split of partner revenue. An EPS beat achieved mainly by fewer shares would carry less weight than better recurring operating economics.

The full-year guide implies $344.7 million of fourth-quarter adjusted operating income when its midpoint is reduced by first-half results and the third-quarter midpoint. That is about 5.3% above fiscal 2026’s fourth-quarter result, not a requirement for an unprecedented holiday acceleration. But the implied number includes the new guide’s assumptions about refunds and partner revenue. We would not call it a pure retail-growth hurdle. An earnings model must keep that distinction through the final quarter, rather than forgetting it after the initial announcement.
