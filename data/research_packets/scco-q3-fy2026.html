Different assets, different operating tests

Southern Copper is an integrated producer spanning Peruvian operations, Mexican open-pit operations and the IMMSA unit. The most recent detailed segment statements reviewed are for Q2 2026. They show that the Mexican open-pit business and Peru account for most sales and operating profit. Those tables provide useful context about the asset base, but their June date stays visible. We do not label them as September segment results or infer current profit by multiplying a prior margin by new revenue.

The current production disclosure tells a more immediate story. Mined copper was 232,396 tonnes. The year-earlier table shows 234,892, establishing a decline of approximately 1.1% year over year. The previous quarter separately reported 230,662 tonnes, so the calculated sequential change is positive 0.751749%, approximately 0.8%. The current release's prose calls the negative 1.1% comparison quarter over quarter; that label conflicts with the table and is retained in the source audit rather than repeated as fact.

Mexico's current mined-copper output increased 2.5% while Peru's declined 6.1%, with lower ore grades cited in Peru. This is a reminder that a consolidated dollar result can conceal different physical operating outcomes. Grades, recoveries, stripping requirements and plant reliability determine how much saleable metal emerges from the ore. Stronger smelting and refining throughput is relevant, but it is not interchangeable with mined copper tonnage.

We therefore separate three tests: the existing operations must sustain physical performance, the processing system must convert material efficiently, and new projects must meet cost and schedule commitments. A better copper price can obscure weaknesses in the first two tests for a period. It cannot remove the need to invest or guarantee that a greenfield project will become productive on its planned date.

Nine-cent cash cost is not the gross cost

The striking headline is operating cash cost after by-product credits: the reconciliation shows 9.5 cents per pound for Q3, while the narrative rounds that to nine cents. Before those credits, operating cash cost was 245.5 cents per pound compared with 222.7 cents a year earlier. The gross measure increased 10.2%. A headline about almost-free copper production can therefore create the wrong impression if the gross-cost bridge is omitted.

The current reconciliation starts with GAAP cost of sales of $1,599.1 million and adjusts for selling expenses, treatment and refining charges, worker participation, third-party concentrates, other charges and inventory movements. It reaches $1,220.1 million of cash cost before by-products. By-product revenues of $1,172.9 million reduce the net measure to $47.2 million. That means by-products offset approximately 96.1% of the gross cash-cost measure. The benefit depends on prices and saleable quantities of other metals; it is not a permanent contractual subsidy.

Adjusted EBITDA was $3,040.2 million. Its reconciliation begins with attributable income, adds minority income, taxes, interest expense and depreciation, then deducts affiliate earnings and interest income. It is a useful operating comparison when the definition is kept consistent. It is not cash available for dividends, because tax, investment and financing claims remain relevant.

Our valuation keeps these distinctions visible. We do not add the by-product benefit a second time after starting from consolidated cash generation. We also do not treat EBITDA as equity cash flow. The key operating checkpoint is whether gross costs and copper output improve while credits remain favorable, rather than relying on the net-cost number alone. A reversal in by-product prices can raise the net measure even with unchanged mine efficiency.

A cash model must distinguish collections and payment timing from a permanent improvement in earning power.

The tax burden also deserves attention. The quarter's $990.9 million tax expense divided by $2,780.1 million pretax income gives an effective rate of 35.6%. Southern Copper's geographic and royalty-tax context makes it inappropriate to apply a generic low corporate tax rate without a bridge. Our cash assumptions are after tax; the operating multiple route is merely a cross-check, not a mechanism to ignore taxation.

End-balance receivables imply about 47.9 days of quarterly sales, using a 92-day quarter. Inventory implies about 61.0 days of cost of sales under the same simplified convention. The downside is more than one copper-price number

Commodity price is the first risk because the quarter's improvement depends heavily on the price environment. A simultaneous decline in copper and by-product prices could reduce revenue while increasing the reported net cash-cost measure. Those effects are linked rather than independent. The second risk is physical execution: grade declines, recoveries, availability and stripping can change how much cash is produced at a given metal price. Strong consolidated revenue can conceal a weaker production trend.

Third is capital intensity. Expansion spending arrives before the cash from new operations, and a delay can extend that funding period. A cash model that adds project output without its investment burden would overstate owner value. Our base model excludes disputed El Pilar contributions and carries a substantial capex assumption. That treatment is deliberately narrower than capitalizing every announced development project at full promised capacity.

Fourth is political and community exposure. Mexico and Peru provide the operating footprint, labor base, permits and infrastructure on which the business depends. Specific access or electricity constraints deserve more weight than a generic country-risk label. Fifth is control: the parent ownership structure can influence capital allocation, related-party arrangements and the timing of distributions. A public listing does not give minority holders operating control.

The bull case is economically coherent. Copper scarcity and investment demand could sustain favorable prices while operations stabilize and Tia Maria advances. In our illustrative $200 case, analyst EPS of $8 earns a twenty-five-times multiple. The $85 bear case uses $5 at seventeen times. These are conditional scenarios, not floors or ceilings. Severe disruption can produce results outside the range, and favorable conditions can last longer than a conservative model assumes. High uncertainty is why a forecast margin of safety matters even when the assets are attractive.
