  1. Productivity improved with the fleet. Rental revenue rose 17.8%, including a 6.5% fleet-productivity contribution. That measure combines rates, utilization and mix; it is not a pure pricing claim.
  2. Specialty is doing more of the work. Quarterly Specialty rental revenue reached $1.704B and rental gross profit $811M, while General Rentals also expanded its margin.

The release clock is unconfirmed in this review; the SEC acceptance time establishes filing receipt, not the press release's publication time. The earnings call occurred the following morning. We do not use the call time to relabel the release or attribute a day's move to one event.

The print — a real acceleration

Growth improved at both the revenue and earnings lines.

Quarterly revenue reached $4.893 billion, up 15.7% from $4.229 billion. Equipment rentals contributed $4.316 billion, up 17.8%. Net income rose from $701 million to $920 million, while diluted GAAP earnings per share increased from $10.91 to $14.77. The difference between profit growth and per-share growth partly reflects a smaller weighted diluted share base, not just better operations. We separate the two effects instead of treating every dollar of EPS improvement as organic growth.

Adjusted EPS was $15.55, compared with $11.70 a year earlier. It is useful for comparing acquisition-accounting and restructuring effects, but it is not a substitute for the GAAP statement. The reconciliation later in this packet retains the individual after-tax exclusions. Revenue, gross profit, operating profit, interest, other income and tax all remain visible. Sales of rental equipment generated $348 million of revenue, while new-equipment sales, contractor supplies and service revenue account for the remainder of consolidated sales.

Management increased annual revenue guidance to $18.0–18.2 billion from $17.5–17.8 billion. Adjusted EBITDA guidance moved to $8.3–8.4 billion from $7.975–8.125 billion. These are meaningful increases, but the corresponding equipment-investment and operating-cash changes must be read alongside them. The release does not provide a new numerical adjusted-EPS range, so our earnings valuation input is an explicit analyst assumption, not disguised management guidance.

The nine-quarter record is intentionally seasonal. Fourth-quarter equipment purchases and cash realization can differ substantially from the middle of the year. Comparing Q3 with the same quarter is more informative than treating sequential growth as a constant run rate. Segments — Specialty carries more weight

Compare rental economics with rental economics.

General Rentals and Specialty both improved, but they are not interchangeable businesses. General Rentals produced $2.612 billion of equipment-rental revenue and $1.003 billion of rental gross profit. Specialty produced $1.704 billion and $811 million, respectively. Specialty's faster expansion increased its share of rental revenue to 39.5%, computed from the reported segment values. That shift helps explain why a consolidated growth rate alone is insufficient.

General Rentals revenue increased 8.8% year over year and its rental gross margin reached 38.4%, versus 36.7%. Specialty revenue grew 34.7% and its margin rose to 47.6%, versus 45.1%. The improvement was therefore broader than one segment's revenue contribution. However, segment rental gross profit is not consolidated operating profit: it excludes other revenue categories and costs below rental gross profit. The packet does not label it EBIT or assign it an unsupported enterprise-value multiple.

Reported fleet productivity increased 6.5%. The company defines productivity through rental rates, time utilization and mix. It should never be paraphrased as a 6.5% price increase. The rental-growth discussion also identifies average original equipment cost growth, estimated fleet inflation and ancillary or re-rent contributions. These components are rounded explanatory measures, not a license to construct a precise organic-growth reconciliation beyond what the issuer reports.

A stronger Specialty mix can support margins and customer relevance, yet it also creates execution tests. New assets must be deployed, maintained and rented effectively, while specialized capacity can carry different demand cycles. Our next-quarter checks focus on both productivity and Specialty margin. Continued revenue growth with falling rental gross margin would weaken the claim that investment is earning attractive incremental returns. Segment and productivity tables provide the direct source; no standalone segment cash-flow split is disclosed here.

Profit — trace the bridge

Keep operating performance and adjustments visible.

Consolidated gross profit was $2.046 billion. After $511 million of selling, general and administrative expense, a $15 million restructuring charge and $111 million of non-rental depreciation and amortization, operating income was $1.409 billion. Interest expense of $184 million was partly offset by $6 million of other income. Pretax income of $1.231 billion less $311 million of taxes produced the $920 million net result. This full path matters because EBITDA removes expenses that equipment owners must eventually fund.

The company reported adjusted EBITDA of $2.331 billion. The reconciliation begins with net income and adds taxes, interest and depreciation to reach EBITDA, then includes the specified restructuring, stock-compensation and fleet-accounting adjustments. We preserve those items instead of calling adjusted EBITDA operating cash. Stock compensation is a recurring economic cost even when excluded from this non-GAAP subtotal. Our enterprise-value multiple is deliberately assessed alongside a cash-yield route and an earnings route that do not mechanically reward every add-back.

The per-share bridge from $14.77 GAAP EPS to $15.55 adjusted EPS adds acquisition-related intangible amortization, acquired-fleet depreciation effects, the fleet fair-value mark-up and restructuring. These are after-tax per-share items; they cannot be added to pretax dollar expenses without a separate reconciliation. The quarterly gap is $0.78 per share. The release's full table, including zero or immaterial categories and the applied tax rate, remains in the dataset.

Year-to-date comparisons also contain business-disposal and terminated-deal effects. The 2026 disposal benefit added $39 million after tax and $53 million to adjusted EBITDA over nine months. That does not make it a Q3 operating benefit. The historical H&E transaction was terminated, and related 2025 benefits affect comparisons rather than representing a pending acquisition today. Investors should distinguish durable rental earnings from these specific events. The current 10-Q and release reconciliations anchor this treatment.

Earnings quality — nine checks

A strong profit result still needs clean conversion.

Cash conversion looks strong at first glance: quarterly operating cash of $1.629 billion exceeded net income, and nine-month operating cash reached $4.934 billion. The distinction is what remains after the fleet and other assets are funded. Equipment rental is capital intensive; depreciation is a cost-allocation measure while purchase payments reflect timing and growth commitments. A high operating-cash-to-profit ratio alone does not establish high distributable cash or prove that maintenance spending is below depreciation.

Receivables reached $3.096 billion. Our simple ending-balance measure is 56.9 days, calculated as receivables divided by quarterly sales times 90. It is a consistency check, not the company's reported collection metric, and seasonality can move it. Inventory is much smaller than rental equipment and should not be confused with the fleet. A retail-style inventory-turn interpretation would miss the principal asset deployment question for this business.

The tax picture warrants attention. Nine-month book tax expense was $735 million, while cash taxes paid were $228 million and deferred taxes increased by $488 million. The gap contributes to current cash conversion but does not by itself imply aggressive accounting. Tax timing can reverse, and fleet investment has substantial tax consequences. Our base case uses the issuer's full-year cash outlook rather than assuming the unusually favorable cash-to-book-tax relationship persists forever.

No separate noncontrolling-interest deduction appears in the reviewed consolidated income statement. That is a statement-level observation, not a claim that every subsidiary has identical legal restrictions. The parent and URNA financing structure still matters. The nine checks below preserve their standard labels and identify the applicable limitation rather than forcing every indicator into a green score. No fraud inference is drawn from these operating measures; an uncalibrated distress score would be inappropriate evidence for this rental-company valuation.

Cash — the extra profit has a job

Investment timing explains why guidance does not translate one-for-one.

Nine-month issuer free cash flow reconciles exactly: $4.934 billion of operating cash, less $4.339 billion of cash rental-equipment purchases and $280 million of other equipment and intangible purchases, plus $1.028 billion of rental sales, $44 million of other asset sales and $34 million of insurance proceeds, equals $1.421 billion. This definition includes the recurring disposal of used rental equipment. Calling the whole amount cash after gross investment without identifying those proceeds would overstate the comparison with a simple operating-cash-minus-capex measure.

Gross rental capital expenditures were $4.746 billion over nine months, above cash rental purchases by $407 million. The difference is timing and measurement; the two figures are not interchangeable. The annual outlook explicitly gives gross and net rental investment. Net rental capex guidance rose to $3.75–4.05 billion, while operating-cash guidance rose to $6.25–6.85 billion. The free-cash-flow range excluding restructuring remains $2.15–2.45 billion. More operating cash is being put to work, not automatically distributed.

Program repurchases of $1.125 billion and dividends of $371 million total $1.496 billion, exceeding reported nine-month FCF by $75 million. This is a useful funding test, not a claim of distress. The cash-flow statement's common-stock repurchase line is larger because it includes withholding-related purchases, and acquisitions, business-sale proceeds, debt movements and cash balances also affect funding. We do not silently merge the program figure with that broader accounting line or claim that a single debt draw financed a specific distribution.

The debt carrying amount was $14.671 billion against $153 million of cash, producing computed net debt of $14.518 billion. Management's 1.8× leverage metric uses its adjusted-EBITDA convention. Liquidity of $2.615 billion provides flexibility, but unused credit is not cash already earned. The October 23 preliminary notes filing is a financing development to monitor; blank amount or coupon fields are not completed terms. Cash and guidance reconciliations and debt disclosures support the distinction.

Valuation — three routes, one discipline

Assumptions are explicit and all arithmetic is reproducible.

Our fair-value anchor is $980 per share, with a $760–$1,225 scenario range and High uncertainty. The $1,098.88 reference price is above the anchor by an amount that is meaningful for a cyclical, capital-intensive business. We rate the shares HOLD with 3/5 conviction. That expresses a balanced view of improving operations and a demanding entry price. It is not a forecast that the stock must fall to the anchor, nor does a strong quarter eliminate uncertainty about future utilization or equipment returns.

The earnings route applies 20 times an analyst-selected $52 normalized adjusted EPS assumption, producing $1,040. The assumption is modestly above the approximate $49.11 sum of the last four reported adjusted quarterly EPS figures and near the provider's current-year estimate. Acquisition-related add-backs remain a limitation, so we do not let this route stand alone. The 20× multiple is a judgment about durability and cyclicality, not an observed industry law or a company target.

The enterprise route applies 9× to the $8.35 billion adjusted-EBITDA guidance midpoint, subtracts $14.518 billion of net debt and divides by 61.990795 million September-end common shares. The result is $978.08. The equity-cash route capitalizes $2.30 billion of guidance free cash flow at a 4% yield, then divides by the same share count, producing $927.56. FCF is already after financing costs; debt is not subtracted a second time in this equity route.

At the reference price, the modeled enterprise value is $82.64 billion, or 9.90× guidance EBITDA. To support that enterprise value at our 9× multiple would require about $9.18 billion of EBITDA. A 4% equity cash yield would require $2.72 billion of cash, above the current midpoint. These are alternative market-implied tests, not simultaneous forecasts. A purchase near $830 with intact operating evidence would provide roughly a 15.3% margin below our anchor.

Management — capital allocation is the test

Measure commitments against cash and reported investment.

Management deserves credit for raising the operating outlook while delivering broader rental-margin improvement. General Rentals and Specialty both contributed, and the fleet-productivity measure improved. The more difficult judgment is how much of that strength becomes enduring cash after equipment spending. We score the operating result positively while keeping the unchanged free-cash-flow range as an open test, rather than assuming that a larger fleet automatically creates proportionate shareholder value.

The announced annual repurchase plan is $1.5 billion, with $1.125 billion completed over nine months. Repurchases can support per-share results, but their economics depend on purchase price and the investment alternatives. They should not be counted as a separate source of operating growth. The quarterly dividend announced with results is $1.97 per share, payable November 25 to holders of record November 11. Future dividends remain discretionary and subject to the business and financing position.

The relevant listed security is United Rentals, Inc. common stock on the New York Stock Exchange. United Rentals North America is a wholly owned operating and financing subsidiary; it is not a second publicly traded common equity whose debt offering should be mistaken for the covered security. The proxy provides one vote per common share. Ernst & Young audited the annual financial statements and has served as auditor since 1997. Ownership disclosures carry their own reference dates, so this packet does not portray older institutional filings as current trading positions.

The annual filing shows limited customer concentration but meaningful supplier concentration in capital spending. The largest customer represented 1% of revenue and the top ten 5%; the largest supplier represented 11% of capex and the top ten 52%. A broad customer base helps, but it does not eliminate common exposure to construction activity, infrastructure spending or equipment availability. Management's capital-allocation judgment is best assessed across those operating constraints and the cash results, not just the size of the buyback authorization.

Risks — rank what can change the conclusion

The strong bull case and the credible bear case both matter.

The first risk is that investment outpaces the durable rental opportunity. A fleet purchased into strong activity may take longer to generate returns if project timing shifts or utilization weakens. The current productivity result is encouraging, but it mixes price, time utilization and fleet composition. A reversal could pressure cash and margins at the same time. We assign this risk high impact and medium likelihood because it directly connects the current growth strategy to the value owners ultimately receive.

The second risk is valuation. At a premium price, a good operating result may be insufficient if the cash outlook remains flat. Multiple compression can offset earnings growth even without a recession. Our sensitivity grid demonstrates how a one-turn change in the enterprise multiple changes equity value after debt. It is deliberately simple and holds debt and shares fixed; actual repurchases, borrowing or acquisitions could amplify or reduce those outcomes.

The third risk is the residual value and funding cycle. Used-equipment sales contribute both profit and cash, and the issuer's FCF definition includes the proceeds. Lower recovery values could reduce the cash contribution from selling equipment while new purchases still need funding. Interest and refinancing remain relevant even with a manageable reported leverage ratio. The preliminary notes filing is monitored as financing evidence, with no guessed final terms. Ordinary financing or a subsidiary transaction does not automatically disqualify the continuing listed parent from analysis.

The strongest bull argument is that Specialty demand and higher fleet productivity make the larger asset base more valuable than a near-term cash snapshot suggests. The strongest bear argument is that incremental profit keeps requiring incremental investment, leaving owners with a thin cash yield at today's price. Both arguments fit portions of the evidence. Our HOLD resolves that tension through explicit price and operating thresholds, while leaving room to update when the next cash and productivity data arrive.

Catalysts — what earns a changed view

Concrete thresholds replace an open-ended optimism test.

The next major operating checkpoint is the Q4 and full-year 2026 release, expected in January 2027; an exact issuer date is not assumed here. We will compare full-year free cash flow excluding restructuring with the current $2.15–2.45 billion guide, and look for a result at or above the $2.30 billion midpoint. That test uses the same definition as guidance. Reported FCF must first be reconciled for restructuring cash payments rather than compared on mismatched bases.

Fleet productivity is the next operating test. We view at least 4% as constructive and below 2% as a warning under the current investment plan. Specialty rental gross margin at or above 46% would support the case that growth remains productive; below 43% would demand a closer examination of mix, deployment and pricing. These are analyst monitoring thresholds, not forecasts, and they should be evaluated alongside the company's explanations and any changes in the measurement basis.

Capital spending provides a third checkpoint. Net rental capex at or below the $3.90 billion midpoint, with growth intact, would strengthen confidence in conversion. Spending above the $4.05 billion top end without a corresponding cash uplift would weaken it. A lower capex number caused by collapsing demand would not be an automatic positive. We also monitor receivable days and leverage so that a temporarily favorable headline cash result does not obscure collections or borrowing changes.

Price matters independently of the operating score. Around $830, the same business assumptions would offer a larger margin below our $980 value anchor. Conversely, a meaningful cash-guide reduction or productivity deterioration could reduce the anchor itself. We would not simply buy because the price touched a number while ignoring the changed evidence. Appendix — definitions and audit trail

Reported facts, computed measures and analyst assumptions remain distinguishable.

This packet covers the three and nine months ended September 30, 2026. Financial statements and release tables are in U.S. dollars, generally millions except per-share data. The nine-quarter series runs from Q3 2024 through Q3 2026. Every quarterly income, adjusted-EPS, adjusted-EBITDA, segment and cash observation is taken from a retained primary release or filing. The earlier mechanical YTD-difference draft is preserved as research history, but the published quarter cash series uses directly reported three-month release values. Balance sheets remain point-in-time observations.

Issuer FCF equals operating cash minus cash purchases of rental equipment and other equipment or intangibles, plus the specified equipment-sale and insurance proceeds. That differs from a gross-capex-only measure. The guidance series excludes restructuring cash payments, whereas the reported series includes them. Gross rental capex and cash rental purchases differ in timing. Adjusted EPS and adjusted EBITDA also have different exclusion structures; neither is casually called GAAP or operating cash. Fleet productivity is a combined rate, utilization and mix measure, while net leverage uses the company's adjusted-EBITDA convention.

Computed ratios include margins, ending-receivable days, net debt, the valuation routes and scenario weights. Our enterprise valuation uses September-end actual common shares rather than the quarterly diluted weighted-average denominator. The approximate trailing EPS measure sums reported quarterly EPS and therefore reflects changing share weights. Model choices, including multiples, yield requirements, scenario probabilities, thresholds and the $52 earnings assumption, are editorial judgments. They are stored separately from reported figures in the underlying dataset and are not presented as issuer or consensus forecasts.

Market data are dated provider observations. The October 23 close is the common valuation reference; the October 26 quote and unfinished daily candle are retained but excluded from the closing chart. Historical return windows are broad event-period observations, not causal attribution. No prior Q2 packet signpost grid was found, so there is no invented historical grading result. The prior episode's angle is documented only to ensure the present analysis addresses the current evidence. Sources, hashes, table locators and complete reconciliations are retained locally for re-verification.
