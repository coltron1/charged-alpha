Five things to know

  1. Revenue and earnings diverged. Innovative Medicine grew faster than MedTech, but research spending, transaction costs and mix weighed on profit.
  2. Separate the legal charge from the business. The $5.519B litigation adjustment is excluded from adjusted profit; possible settlement cash remains economically relevant.
  3. Tremfya replaces much of the Stelara decline. Their combined sales increased only $118M; the rest of the portfolio must keep contributing.
  4. The cash story needs the filing. Management estimates more than $6B of quarterly free cash flow, while detailed Q3 cash-flow and balance-sheet tables are still pending.
  5. Do not pay for every pipeline success today. The $235 anchor is below the $253.62 dated price; new indications and portfolio changes must earn their valuation.

The print

The cleanest comparison is growing sales against falling adjusted earnings.

Johnson & Johnson reported $25.399 billion of sales, up 5.9% from $23.993 billion. Operational growth was 6.0%, and adjusted operational growth was 6.1%. Those are different measures: operational growth removes currency effects, while adjusted operational growth also reflects specified portfolio effects. We retain the reported dollars for the financial model rather than mixing adjusted growth with a reported revenue base.

GAAP earnings were only $210 million, or nine cents a share, versus $5.152 billion a year ago. The legal charge explains much of that dramatic reduction. But removing designated items still leaves adjusted net income of $6.339 billion, down 6.8%, and adjusted EPS of $2.59, down 7.5%. It would be misleading to dismiss the entire earnings decline as a litigation accounting event. The business also has a real cost and mix challenge inside the adjusted result.

A reliable quarter-specific consensus snapshot was unavailable from the subscribed estimates endpoint. We therefore do not label the report an EPS beat or miss using a reconstructed number. That distinction matters because an apparent beat can depend on the date and definition of the estimate, especially when acquisitions change which costs analysts exclude.

Segments

Product replacement is working, but it is not free.

Innovative Medicine generated $16.757 billion of revenue, up 7.7%, while MedTech delivered $8.642 billion, up 2.5%. Medicine supplied roughly two-thirds of consolidated sales and most of the dollar growth. This is a diversified healthcare group, but the growth burden is not evenly distributed. The central operating question is whether the next set of medicines can replace patent erosion while supporting attractive research returns.

Those are valuable growth engines, but a broad product list should not obscure concentration: a few successful franchises carry a significant part of the valuation. Tremfya rose by $972 million while Stelara fell by $854 million, leaving a combined increase of $118 million. Profit bridge

A large adjustment does not erase an economic obligation.

The quarter’s $34 million of GAAP pretax income becomes $7.672 billion after the company’s designated pretax adjustments. The largest is $5.519 billion of litigation expense. Other adjustments include $1.240 billion of intangible amortization, $339 million of in-process research impairments, $369 million related to the Orthopaedics separation and $195 million of restructuring-related costs. Acquisition and integration adjustments were a net $34 million credit, so they reduce rather than increase this bridge.

The tax line is a $176 million benefit against just $34 million of pretax income. Dividing those two produces a negative 517.6% GAAP effective rate, a mathematically correct but economically unhelpful run-rate assumption. The adjusted rate is 17.4%. Neither the GAAP benefit nor the litigation add-back should be capitalized as recurring operating improvement. The earnings route uses a dated adjusted consensus and is checked against cash rather than assuming that every excluded cost is irrelevant.

The company describes a proposed $5.5 billion ovarian-talc resolution conditioned on at least 95% participation in remaining claims. A booked expense and a cash payment are different events. The charge is already reflected in reported profit; the future timing, conditions and funding matter for shareholder cash. We do not subtract the same charge from both earnings and valuation indiscriminately, but we also do not present adjusted profit as if the associated obligation disappears.

Intangible amortization is noncash in the current period, but the acquisitions that created those assets cost real money. Adding amortization back can help compare current operating activity; it does not prove that the original purchase price earned an adequate return. The same caution applies to repeated portfolio charges. A sequence of individually unusual costs can become a persistent burden for an acquisitive company.

The cash-conversion chip remains unavailable for the exact quarter because management’s free-cash-flow estimate does not provide the full operating-cash reconciliation. Receivables and inventory are shown through Q2 only. We do not manufacture a current accruals score or a fraud-screening statistic from mixed dates, nor treat an unavailable score as a pass. The useful next check is the filed cash statement: identify litigation payments, acquisition effects and working-capital movements before judging whether the earnings rebound is durable.

Cash and balance sheet

Strong recurring cash, substantial competing uses.

Operating cash flow was $24.530 billion in 2025 and capital expenditure was $4.832 billion, leaving $19.698 billion of free cash flow. That was slightly below 2024’s $19.842 billion despite higher operating cash flow, because investment spending increased. The three-year history supports the existence of a large cash engine. It does not support treating every dollar as available for buybacks, since dividends, acquisitions, debt management and legal obligations compete for that cash.

Valuation

Three routes converge near $234; the rounded anchor is $235.

We value the continuing parent common share in U.S. dollars. The DCF uses an equity cash-flow convention: operating cash flow less capital expenditure is already after the interest burden embedded in CFO. We therefore discount it at an equity required return and do not subtract net debt a second time. This is a deliberately transparent recurring-cash model, not a full acquisition-financing model. A material settlement payment or new deal could reduce the cash available to shareholders and would require an explicit update.

1

Recurring equity cash flow: $223

Start with management’s approximately $21B 2026 FCF guide. Grow by 8% annually for five years; discount at 8%; use 3% terminal growth. Divide the resulting equity cash value by 2.4099B shares implied by the dated market cap and price. No second net-debt deduction.

Dated earnings path: $249

Terminal growth begins after the explicit five-year period. A large share of value sits beyond that period, so small rate changes materially affect the answer.

Eight percent explicit cash growth assumes that replacement medicines and MedTech execution can grow distributable cash faster than a mature low-growth pharmaceutical basket. The matching 8% required return reflects the franchise’s resilience without treating it as a bond. Three percent terminal growth is below the explicit period and assumes long-run reinvestment sustains a durable business. None of these is an issuer forecast. The grid shows how quickly value falls if the required return rises or long-run growth disappoints.

The simple mean of the three routes is $233.81, rounded to a $235 anchor. The scenarios deliberately span $190 to $285, and their weighted value is $236.25. These are distinct outputs. At $253.62, the anchor implies about 7.3% downside before dividends. With high uncertainty, a 20% discount to the anchor would put a stronger entry margin near $188. That is a valuation discipline rather than a forecast that the stock must trade there.

The market-implied test is demanding even without a reverse model: the price asks investors to accept only about a 3.44% yield on the approximately $21 billion FCF guide, with growth doing the rest. Our 8%/8%/3% cash model produces less than the market price. To justify more, an investor needs higher sustained cash growth, a lower required return or a more generous terminal assumption. It is reasonable to believe in the pipeline; the unresolved question is how much of that success has already been paid for.

Management raised and narrowed its annual outlook while acknowledging an unfavorable near-term mix of costs. The guidance change is small relative to the size of the business: the reported sales midpoint increased by $100 million and adjusted EPS by six cents. Those increases are useful evidence of confidence, but they should be weighed against the actual current-quarter adjusted decline. We would give more credit to a sustained improvement in cash returns than to a higher adjusted target by itself.

The intended DePuy Synthes separation remains an analysis topic, not a reason to exclude the continuing parent from coverage. Management is evaluating alternatives and says it is leaning toward a spin. There is no basis here to assume final distribution ratios, proceeds, retained debt or tax treatment. A sum-of-parts calculation that assigns the entire separation benefit today while leaving every current earnings dollar in the parent would double count value. Our present model keeps the reported perimeter and flags the future change.

  1. How much of the approximately $21B FCF guide will remain after separately identified legal payments and transaction cash uses?
  2. What precise assumptions support the $1.36 per-share 2027 deal dilution, and when should the acquired programs begin generating returns?
  3. Can Tremfya plus Stelara sustain combined growth while other immunology products decline?
  4. What debt, pension, tax and stranded-cost allocations will accompany a DePuy separation?
  5. When will adjusted margin improvement translate into higher per-share cash flow rather than only a larger list of exclusions?

The annual filings report approximately 131,900 employees for 2023, 138,100 for 2024 and 138,200 for 2025. The provider profile carries a different current number, so the historical table uses the filings and does not imply a like-for-like productivity gain from mixed dates. Management’s capital-allocation record should be judged on returns after those investments, not simply on the number of programs added to the pipeline.

The bull case deserves a fair hearing. Oncology and immunology growth can outweigh erosion, the group can fund innovation from substantial internal cash, and a well-designed separation could sharpen accountability. If the new portfolio produces better growth and returns while legal uncertainty declines, a premium multiple can be justified. The bull scenario is not merely a higher target; it requires those operating and cash conditions to occur together.

The bear case is more than a litigation headline. Adjusted profit can remain under pressure even after the special charge falls away. Acquisition dilution, research spending, pricing constraints and slower replacement growth could leave per-share cash growth below what the stock already discounts. A separation can also create costs or reduce diversification before delivering benefits. Under that path, a lower multiple and lower sustainable earnings reinforce each other.

Our HOLD conclusion sits between those cases. The business has real strengths and a long cash-generation record, but the current price offers limited room for several uncertainties to resolve unfavorably. This is a valuation judgment with medium conviction, not a declaration that the company is broken or that every transaction destroys value. New filed cash data, clearer separation economics or a materially different price can change the conclusion.

Structure and methods: JNJ is the NYSE-listed U.S. common share, not an ADR, and no ADS conversion is used. The annual auditor is PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP. No VIE or dual-class discount is inserted without an identified structure requiring one. The market-implied share count is market capitalization divided by the same dated price; diluted shares are weighted-average earnings denominators. The DCF is an equity cash-flow model, while net debt is shown separately as a balance-sheet risk. Product concentration is disclosed through the named-franchise revenue table; product sales do not establish customer concentration.

The income exhibit’s cost of products sold includes intangible amortization. A provider classification that removes it would overstate the GAAP gross margin used here. Similarly, debt amounts are taken from the actual balance-sheet carrying-value rows rather than rounded debt-note tags.
